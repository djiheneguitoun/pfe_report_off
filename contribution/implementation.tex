\section{Introduction}%
\label{sec:impl:intro}

\Cref{chap:system} described the system at the level of its design: what each
component does, what it produces, and why it is built that way. A design of this
kind leaves several things to be settled. The components have to be realised
with concrete tools. The simulator, the flight software and the decision code
have to be organised so that they run together. The learned elements have to be
trained before they can be used. Once the system exists, it must also be shown
that it does what the design promises. This chapter addresses both aspects, in
two parts.

The first part covers the implementation. It begins with the technologies and
libraries on which the system is built, and with the machine on which the
missions are run. It then presents the technical architecture, which organises
the simulated warehouse, the vehicles, their flight software and the components
of \Cref{chap:system} into layers, from the physics of the simulation up to the
records produced by a mission. Finally, it presents the learned detector and the
semantic guide.

The second part covers the evaluation. It first defines the protocol, with the
scenarios, the reference methods and the metrics. It then evaluates the main
components, before reporting the results of the missions against the reference
methods. The chapter closes with a conclusion that brings these results together.

\textbf{NVIDIA Isaac Sim}\footnote{\url{https://developer.nvidia.com/isaac/sim}}

\textbf{Pegasus
    Simulator}\footnote{\url{https://pegasussimulator.github.io/PegasusSimulator/}}

The \textbf{Iris quadrotor} is the vehicle model used for every drone. It is a
four-rotor airframe widely used as a reference in drone simulation.

\textbf{ArduPilot}\footnote{\url{https://ardupilot.org}} is the autopilot

system.

\textbf{MAVLink}, used through the
\textbf{pymavlink}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ArduPilot/pymavlink}}

\textbf{Sionna}\footnote{\url{https://nvlabs.github.io/sionna/}} is NVIDIA's

\section{Technical Architecture}%
\label{sec:impl:architecture}

The system is evaluated entirely in simulation, but the simulation is designed to
reproduce the conditions of a real deployment. Each drone is flown by genuine
autopilot software, and the system knows only what the drones' own sensors
measure. This requires several programs to run concurrently and to exchange data.
\Cref{fig:impl:architecture} presents their organisation.

\subsection*{Layer 1 --- Simulated World}

Isaac Sim provides the physical world in which the missions take place. It
computes the motion of the rigid bodies, renders the images seen by the cameras
and simulates the \gls{abb:lidar} measurements. The scene, with the building, the
racks and the cartons, is described in USD (Universal Scene Description), the
scene format used by Isaac Sim.

The warehouse is not a fixed model. It is produced by a generator from a single
integer, the \emph{seed}. The same seed always yields the same warehouse, while a
different seed yields a different layout. The seed determines the position of
each rack, the fraction of shelf positions that hold a carton and the take-off
positions of the drones. The dimensions of the building and of the racks remain
constant (\Cref{tab:impl:warehouse}). This mechanism is what allows the system to
be evaluated in warehouses it has never encountered.

\subsection*{Layer 2 --- Vehicles}

Pegasus Simulator adds the drones to Isaac Sim, and each drone uses the Iris
quadrotor model, a standard airframe in drone simulation. Pegasus also acts as
the bridge between Isaac Sim and the flight software. It transmits the simulated
inertial and position data of each drone to its autopilot, and it applies the
resulting motor commands to the drone in Isaac Sim.

Each drone carries four sensors, configured as shown in \Cref{tab:impl:sensors}:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.35em]
    \item \textbf{Side cameras}: The two side cameras are the reading instruments.
          They point perpendicular to the direction of flight, one to the left and
          one to the right, so that a single pass along an aisle covers the racks on
          both sides. 
    \item \textbf{Front camera}: A low-resolution front camera serves the visual
          variant of the semantic guide.
    \item \textbf{\glsxtrshort{abb:lidar}}: The \gls{abb:lidar} measures the
          distance to the nearest obstacle along a set of rays distributed around
          the drone, and these distances are used to build the map of the building.
         
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Layer 3 --- Flight Software}

Each drone is flown by its own instance of ArduPilot, an open-source autopilot
widely used on real drones. It runs in \glsxtrshort{abb:sitl}
(software-in-the-loop) mode: the flight code executes on the computer as an
ordinary program and receives simulated sensor data instead of real measurements.
The system therefore never sets the position of a drone directly in Isaac Sim. It
sends commands to the autopilot, which flies the drone with the inertia, delays
and small tracking errors of a real aircraft. The behaviour observed in
simulation thus remains close to that of physical drones.

The system communicates with the autopilots through MAVLink, the standard
messaging protocol between an autopilot and a control program. Before a mission,
each drone is connected to its autopilot and waits until the autopilot's position
estimate is reliable. It is then switched to GUIDED mode, in which the autopilot
accepts commands from an external program, before being armed and taking off.
The three drones take off at staggered heights of 1.6\,m, 1.9\,m and 2.2\,m.

During the mission, the flight controller converts the pose selected by the
system (a position and a heading), together with the route leading to it, into
velocity commands for the autopilot. It operates in three successive phases:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.35em]
    \item \textbf{Transit}: The drone follows the waypoints of its route at
          constant speed.
    \item \textbf{Approach}: The speed of the drone decreases in proportion to the
          remaining distance, which prevents it from overshooting into a rack. The
          drone also rotates until its side camera faces the target.
    \item \textbf{Hold}: The drone maintains the pose while the cameras observe. In
          front of a label, the hold lasts 2\,s, which gives ten images to the
          decoder.
\end{itemize}

If the drone makes no progress towards its target for 8\,s, the target is
abandoned and a new one is selected.

\subsection*{Layer 4 --- System}

The components described in \Cref{chap:system} run inside the Isaac Sim process,
within a single loop: perception, the shared map, candidate generation, target
selection and coordination.

Decision-making is decentralised: there is no leader and no central planner, and
every drone runs the same software and selects its own targets. What the drones
share is their knowledge of the building. Each drone writes its observations into
a single common map held by the Isaac Sim process, and reads this map when it
selects a target.

The loop proceeds in cycles of 0.2\,s of simulated time. Within a cycle:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.2em]
    \item the flight controllers send their commands;
    \item Isaac Sim advances the physics and renders the images;
    \item every flying drone integrates its observations into the map;
    \item expired claims are removed;
    \item at most one drone selects a new target.
\end{enumerate}

Each drone thus receives five observations per second. Since a single drone
decides in each cycle, the computation spent on decisions does not increase with
the size of the team.

\subsection*{Layer 5 --- Learned Models}

Two learned models run on the GPU in support of the system:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.35em]
    \item \textbf{The learned detector} (\Cref{sec:impl:perception}), from the YOLO11 family,
          locates \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} labels and cartons in the images, including
          labels too distant for their content to be decoded. It processes one side
          camera per cycle, alternating between left and right. The decoder,
          zxing-cpp, extracts the identifiers and processes both images at every
          cycle.
    \item \textbf{The semantic guide} (\Cref{sec:impl:guide}), based on the Qwen2.5-VL
          vision-language model, advises each drone on the area to visit next.
          Producing an answer takes a few seconds, so the guide runs in the
          background. The drone continues its mission in the meantime, and the
          advice is applied to its next decision.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Layer 6 --- Network}

In the system, the observations of all drones converge to a single shared map. In
a real deployment, each drone would therefore have to send its observations over
a wireless link to the point that holds this map. The network part was developed
to evaluate the quality of such links inside the warehouse, where racks and walls
attenuate and block the radio signal. A link always connects two points of the
warehouse, which can be two drones, or a drone and a fixed point such as a base
station. The evaluation relies on two tools with complementary roles:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.35em]
    \item \textbf{Sionna} computes the propagation of the radio signal by ray
          tracing in a three-dimensional model of the warehouse. For the two points
          of a link, it finds the paths followed by the signal, including
          reflections, and derives the received signal strength. When no path
          exists, it reports the link as blocked.
    \item \textbf{ns-3} simulates the communication protocols, from radio access to
          packet transport, and measures the delay with which packets travel from
          one end of a link to the other.
\end{itemize}

Both tools follow the drones during the flight. The positions of the drones are
written continuously to a file, which ns-3 reads to place and move its network
nodes. Test packets are sent between the nodes of the simulated network to
measure latency, while Sionna provides the signal strength of each link. The
ns3sionna module can also make ns-3 take its propagation losses and delays
directly from Sionna. In that case, Sionna runs as a separate server that ns-3
contacts through ZeroMQ messages.

\subsection*{Layer 7 --- Outputs}

Everything that happens during a mission is recorded, and none of these records
is read by the system while the mission runs. A mission record contains:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.2em]
    \item the timestamped events, such as the loss of a drone or the appearance of
          an obstacle;
    \item the number of identifiers read over time;
    \item the decisions, trajectory and attitude of every drone;
    \item a snapshot of the map every 10\,s and the final map;
    \item the camera images from which the mission videos are assembled.
\end{itemize}

Once the mission is over, an automatic judge compares the record with the
\emph{ground truth} of the warehouse, that is, the identifier and true position of
every \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} panel, as produced by the generator. The system never
has access to this ground truth, which is used only by the judge. The judge first
verifies that:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.2em]
    \item no drone remained blocked;
    \item no two drones pursued the same target;
    \item the drones dispersed at the start of the mission;
    \item the loss of a drone, when one occurred, was absorbed by the others;
    \item the mission ended properly.
\end{itemize}

It then measures the identifiers read, the share of the building that became
known and the safety of the flight. All the results reported in \Cref{sec:impl:results} are
computed from these records.

\section{Perception: Decoder and Learned Detector}%
\label{sec:impl:perception}

Reading a code and detecting a label are two different tasks. The decoder reads a
\glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code only within the reading envelope, up to about 4\,m,
while the detector must find labels from farther away so that unexplored areas
remain available to the planner.

\textbf{The decoder}: The decoder was selected experimentally because the reading
range depends on the decoder. A benchmark of 3,481 images was collected with the
exact camera pose recorded, over \textbf{five acquisition campaigns covering
    different warehouses, viewpoints and flight conditions}.

0.5.

\section{The Semantic Guide}%
\label{sec:impl:guide}

The semantic guide is the second learned component. It uses the current state of
the mission to suggest the area a drone should visit next. Its suggestion is not
a command; it is converted into a bonus for candidate targets in the suggested
zone.

\textbf{Two variants}: Two input formats were tested. The visual variant provides
a frontal-camera image and a top view of the map with candidate zones shown as
numbered circles. The model answers two questions: which zone to visit and which
side to approach from. The textual variant provides the state of each candidate
zone as a structured record and asks only for the zone number. The visual variant
is retained for the missions.

\textbf{What the model receives}: The record is built only from the shared map
and contains observed information. It includes the elapsed time, number of
identifiers already read, the position and heading of each drone, and, for each
candidate zone, its centre, unread labels, unobserved surfaces, aisle-facing
side, and distance from the drone. A reduced record removes four fields that did
not improve the choice: total candidate count, target types, zone radius, and
number of unexplored frontier groups. Zone numbers are randomly assigned at every
request so that the model must use the zone information rather than its position.

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{Prompt}: You help a team of drones that must read
    \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} codes glued on cardboard boxes in a warehouse. The drone
    must fly to the zone where it will read the largest number of codes that are
    not read yet.

    \textbf{Question}: Which zone should the drone go to next? Answer with
    \texttt{ANSWER: <zone number>}.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Choice of the model}: The model must run together with Isaac Sim, so
memory usage is an important constraint. Several models were implemented and
measured.

\section{The Reinforcement Learning Approach}%
\label{sec:impl:rl}

\textbf{Principle}: One policy is shared by the three drones. Each drone gives
its current observation to the policy, which directly outputs its velocity
command. The same policy therefore performs navigation, target selection and task
allocation, and no manual rule specifies which \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code or area
should be searched next. Unread-code positions are not given to the policy. They are used only during
training, by the reward and the critic. The critic can therefore use privileged
information, while the final policy only needs onboard observations.

\textbf{Observation}: Each drone receives two $32 \times 32$ crops of the shared
map, centred on the drone and aligned with its heading, each containing nine map
layers:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.2em]
    \item a local crop covering 8\,m;
    \item a larger crop covering 32\,m.
\end{itemize}

It also receives a 98-value vector containing \gls{abb:lidar} information, the
drone's current motion, the relative positions of the other drones, and the task
thresholds.

\textbf{Action}: The policy outputs four velocity values at 30\,Hz, bounded and
normalised before being applied. The policy therefore controls the drone
continuously, rather than selecting from a fixed list of movements.

\textbf{Reward}: The reward combines six objectives:

\begin{equation}
    r = \text{coverage} + \text{\glsxtrshort{abb:qr} readings}
    + \text{collision avoidance} + \text{progress}
    - \text{command changes} - \text{drone proximity}
    \label{eq:impl:reward}
\end{equation}

More precisely:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Coverage}: rewards discovering useful areas and new viewpoints,
          while penalising unnecessary overlap.
    \item \textbf{\glsxtrshort{abb:qr} readings}: gives a strong reward for reading
          a new \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code and a smaller reward for reaching a
          suitable reading posture.
    \item \textbf{Milestones}: additional rewards are given at 50\%, 75\% and 90\%
          team coverage.
    \item \textbf{Collision avoidance}: penalises approaching obstacles, with a
          stronger penalty below 0.25\,m.
    \item \textbf{Progress}: a potential-based term encourages movement toward the
          nearest unread code.
    \item \textbf{Command smoothness}: penalises sudden changes in velocity.
    \item \textbf{Drone separation}: penalises drones that get too close to each
          other.
\end{itemize}

The reward weights change during training:

\begin{equation}
    c(t) = 1 - 0.5t, \qquad l(t) = 1 + 2t
    \label{eq:impl:reward-schedule}
\end{equation}

where $c(t)$ controls the importance of exploration and $l(t)$ the importance of
\glsxtrshort{abb:qr} reading.

\textbf{Training}: PPO is used for training, with one policy shared by all three
drones. Sixty-four warehouses are simulated in parallel, which gives
$64 \times 3 = 192$ parallel drones. Each episode uses:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.2em]
    \item one of 500 fixed warehouse layouts;
    \item different initial positions and headings;
    \item optionally, one randomly selected drone failure between 25\% and 75\% of
          the episode.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Curriculum}: The task becomes gradually more difficult.

and how the three drones cooperate.

\section{The Active Inference Approach}%
\label{sec:impl:aif}

\textbf{Principle}: No policy is trained. Each drone maintains a belief about the
warehouse, evaluates several possible actions, and selects the one with the
lowest expected free energy:

\begin{equation}
    \text{chosen action} = \arg\min_x G(x)
    \label{eq:impl:argmin}
\end{equation}

The score combines:

\begin{equation}
    G = -\text{information} - \text{exploration} + \text{movement cost}
    + \text{drone risk} + \text{obstacle risk}
    \label{eq:impl:efe}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Shared map}: The warehouse is represented by an occupancy grid in which
each cell starts at $p = 0.5$. The \gls{abb:lidar} measurements update the grid:
the crossed cells become more likely to be free, and the hit cell becomes more
likely to be occupied. The three drones exchange their maps, which are fused into
a shared representation, and each drone combines this shared map with its local
map.

\textbf{Uncertainty}: Uncertainty is measured using the occupancy entropy:

\begin{equation}
    H(p) = -p \log p - (1-p) \log (1-p)
    \label{eq:impl:entropy}
\end{equation}

A high $H$ means the cell is uncertain, and a low $H$ that it is well known. The
average entropy $\bar{H}$ represents the uncertainty of the shared map.

\textbf{Candidate actions}: Nine candidates are evaluated, eight directions at
approximately 1\,m and one hover action. A candidate is removed if it lies
outside the navigable area, or if its occupancy is above 0.65.

\textbf{Expected free energy}: Each valid candidate is evaluated using five
terms:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Information gain $IG$}: how much new information the position can
          provide.
    \item \textbf{Frontier term $F$}: preference for uncertain or unexplored areas.
    \item \textbf{Movement cost}: penalises unnecessary movement.
    \item \textbf{Drone collision risk $C$}: penalises positions close to other
          drones.
    \item \textbf{Obstacle risk $Cl$}: strongly penalises positions close to
          occupied cells.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Main weights of the expected free energy.}%
    \label{tab:impl:efe}
    \singlespacing
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{8.0cm}
            >{\raggedleft\arraybackslash}p{3.0cm}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Term}         & \textbf{Weight} \\
        \midrule
        Information gain      & $-2.5$          \\
        Frontier attraction   & $-0.8$          \\
        Movement cost         & $+0.1$          \\
        Drone collision risk  & $+5.0$          \\
        Obstacle risk         & $+25.0$         \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}

The lowest-$G$ action is preferred. During stochastic evaluation, actions are
sampled according to

\begin{equation}
    P(x_i) \propto \exp\Bigl(-\frac{G_i - \min_j G_j}{T}\Bigr), \qquad T = 0.3
    \label{eq:impl:softmax}
\end{equation}

and this randomness helps prevent the three drones from repeatedly choosing the
same action.

\textbf{Change detection}: The system also checks whether the real environment
differs from the current map. New \gls{abb:lidar} observations are compared with
the map prediction, and a large difference produces an alert. The alert starts a
30-cycle recovery phase, during which information gathering receives additional
importance, followed by a durable phase that continues to reduce map uncertainty.
Normal operation resumes when both the map uncertainty and the detected change
become sufficiently low.

\textbf{\glsxtrshort{abb:qr} inventory variant}: For \glsxtrshort{abb:qr}
inspection, the same framework is adapted. Candidates correspond to possible
inspection movements. The epistemic term measures how many previously unscanned
facade cells could become visible, while the pragmatic term measures the distance
to useful inspection positions along the warehouse aisles. Safety and movement
costs remain included. The resulting criterion is:

\begin{equation}
    G = -w_e \, \text{epistemic} - w_p \, \text{pragmatic}
    + w_r \, \text{risk} + \text{movement cost}
    \label{eq:impl:efe-inventory}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Main idea}: the active inference approach does not learn a fixed
behaviour. It continuously updates the warehouse belief and chooses the next
movement according to what can be learned, what can be inspected, and what is
safe.

\section{Conclusion}%
\label{sec:impl:conclusion}

This chapter presented the implementation of SwarmScan and its experimental
evaluation.

The implementation part described the technologies and the execution environment
of the system, then its technical architecture, from the simulator to the
decision layer. Two learned components were evaluated before being integrated.
The learned detector found 98.3\% of the visible labels in two warehouses never
used during its training, and reported a label on only 0.7\% of the images that
contained none. The semantic guide, once specialised, chose the most useful zone
in 96\% of 72 unseen cases, against 83\% for the geometric planner.

The evaluation placed the system in four missions: a nominal mission, the loss of
a vehicle, an obstacle appearing in an aisle, and a warehouse never seen before.
SwarmScan read 96.5\% of the identifiers in the three missions of the reference
warehouse and 92.3\% in the unseen one, the only identifiers missed being carried
by the smallest labels. Against a static approach that plans the whole mission
from a known map, it reached 90\% of the inventory at 195.6\,s instead of
514.8\,s, and flew 1.9 to 2.5 times less to obtain the same result. The loss of a
vehicle left its result unchanged, while the static approach lost 31 identifiers,
and the obstacle cost it eleven seconds and no identifier. Across the four
missions, no vehicle entered a rack, no target was duplicated, and the labels
read were placed in the map with a median error of one centimetre.

Together, these results support the core hypothesis of this work: a team of
drones that builds its own map during the flight and decides from it, without a
central planner, delivers a complete inventory faster than a team following a
plan fixed in advance, and keeps delivering it when the mission does not go as
planned. The general conclusion synthesises these findings and outlines the
directions for future work.
