\section{Problem Formulation}%
\label{sec:sys:problem}

\subsection{The Inspection Task}%
\label{subsec:sys:task}

An inventory inspection compares what is physically present on the shelves with
the records held by the \gls{abb:wms}. Keeping these two views consistent is what
makes the operation necessary, since miscounts and misplaced items reduce order
accuracy and raise operating costs~\cite{pore2026drone}. The task takes as input
a bounded space containing stored items, each carrying a visual identifier on its
faces, and returns the list of identifiers actually found, together with the
position at which each was read. Two properties determine whether that list is
usable. It must account for as large a share of the stored items as possible,
since an item that is not counted is an item the records will continue to report
incorrectly. And it must contain no identifier that does not exist in the
warehouse, since a single spurious entry places the whole list in doubt.

\subsection{Operating Conditions}%
\label{subsec:sys:conditions}

The task is carried out under conditions that no design can change. They are
stated here as facts, and the requirements of the following section follow from
them.

\textbf{The space}: No description of the building is available at take-off. The
number of racks, their position and the location of the items are unknown, and
the free volume is bounded by fixed obstacles, racks, columns and pallets left on
the floor, as well as by moving ones, operators and handling
vehicles~\cite{pore2026drone, pore2026uavqr}. A typical warehouse is a large
building whose racks can exceed ten metres in
height~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}, and the spacing between them is tight enough
to force conservative speed planning~\cite{pore2026drone}. The contents of the
space also change while the mission runs: items are deposited and removed during
the operating day, and an aisle that was clear a minute earlier can be
obstructed. Since neither the layout nor its evolution is known beforehand, no
route through the space and no division of that space between vehicles can be
established before the flight.

\textbf{The reading}: A visual identifier is decodable only within a bounded range
of distances, within a bounded viewing angle and below a bounded relative
speed~\cite{dong2024motionblurred, alsulami2026deep}, so a surface can be
observed without its label being read. Lighting and occlusion narrow that range
further: decoding accuracy falls from 98.5\% in a well-lit environment to 85.2\%
in low light and to 78.3\% when the code is partly
hidden~\cite{alsulami2026deep}, and the motion of the platform produces blur that
lowers the recognition rate~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}. The total number of
items present is not known before the mission, and nothing in the environment
signals that the inventory is complete.

\textbf{The fleet}: The endurance of a single vehicle is bounded, and falls below
what the coverage of a working warehouse demands: small \glspl{abb:uav} typically
fly for twenty to thirty minutes~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}, while a complete
manual inventory check consumes more than three days of warehouse
activity~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}. Several vehicles therefore operate in the
same space, which includes aisles wide enough for only one of them at a time.
Using more than one also shortens the mission, since two drones halved the
inspection time of a single one in a warehouse experiment~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. A
vehicle can stop flying at any moment, from a technical failure or a depleted
battery, and without warning~\cite{chandran2024network}. Perception and decision
run on the same limited onboard computer, and the mission depends on both, a
combination identified in the literature as an open
difficulty~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.

\subsection{Requirements and Constraints}%
\label{subsec:sys:requirements}

From these conditions follow seven results that a solution is expected to
produce. They are stated as outcomes that can be verified on a completed mission
rather than as mechanisms, so that they remain independent of any particular
design.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item The inventory is completed without any description of the layout, any
          route, and any division of work being supplied before take-off.
    \item No identifier is reported that does not correspond to an item present
          in the warehouse.
    \item When a vehicle stops flying, the area it was covering is inspected by
          the remaining vehicles before the mission ends.
    \item Two vehicles do not travel to the same objective, so that the capacity
          of the fleet is not spent twice on a single reading.
    \item No vehicle collides with a surface, with an obstacle that appeared
          after take-off, or with another vehicle.
    \item The mission ends at a moment the system determines by itself, without
          an external instruction, and without spending the remaining flight
          budget on a search that no longer produces readings.
    \item The computation performed for each decision, together with the
          perception running alongside it, remains within the onboard budget and
          within the endurance of the platform.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Position of Existing Approaches}%
\label{subsec:sys:position}

No existing system produces these seven results together. Systems designed for
warehouse inspection receive the layout before take-off and compute their
coverage route once, on the ground, leaving the vehicle to execute
it~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, pore2026uavqr, cristiani2020inventory,
    alsulami2026deep, kwon2020robust, guinand2021uavugv}. What matters is not how
that route is computed but when: the decision is made when the system knows the
least it will ever know, and a target appearing later cannot be taken into
account, because the component that produced the plan is no longer
running~\cite{krayani2023goal}. The same design carries two further assumptions.
The environment is treated as immobile, although operators and handling vehicles
move through a working warehouse, and only about 3\% of inventory studies
consider a dynamic one~\cite{pore2026drone}. And cooperation between flying
vehicles is almost absent: where several vehicles are used, the ground robot
carries the drone or places beacons rather than reading any code
itself~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, guinand2021uavugv}.

The methods that could decide during the flight are not designed for this task.
Those that coordinate several vehicles fix the division of labour before
take-off, by a central schedule~\cite{guinand2021uavugv}, a fixed
split~\cite{pore2026uavqr}, or a leader broadcasting
waypoints~\cite{chandran2024network}, and allocation able to adapt in real time
is still described as a future direction rather than a current
practice~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Semantic reasoning appears in two
separate places, and never where it would be needed. On a ground robot working
alone, it does drive exploration, but the mission solves Object Goal Navigation
and ends as soon as one instance of a designated target is
reached~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm, yu2023l3mvn}, and the representation built for
that target cannot be reused for another~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm}. On a drone, it
only interprets an instruction given by a person: the vehicle carries out what it
has been asked to do and never chooses where to explore~\cite{chen2024typefly}.
Semantic reasoning, cooperation and flight therefore never occur together; three
of the four combinations exist, and the missing one is exactly what an inventory
requires.

The two sides therefore never meet: the systems that perform the right task
cannot react, and the systems that can react were not built for it and stop at
the first success. Four of the seven results are ruled out by that alone. An
inventory cannot be completed without a layout when the layout is what the
planner consumes. The area of a vehicle that stops cannot be reassigned when the
assignment was made once. An obstacle appearing after take-off cannot be avoided
by a route fixed before it existed. And a system that ends at the first detection
cannot decide when an inventory is complete. One caveat applies throughout: these
limitations are established almost entirely on simulated missions, since about
45\% of inventory studies are simulation-based against roughly 7\% that report
real experiments~\cite{pore2026drone}.
\section{Overview of the Proposed Approach}%
\label{sec:sys:overview}

The proposed system performs warehouse inventory inspection in an environment
whose layout is not known in advance. It is a multi-\gls{abb:uav} system: the
inspection is carried out by a team of identical aerial vehicles operating
simultaneously in the same building. Its organisation is decentralised, with no
leader and no central planner; every vehicle runs the same software and reaches
its own decisions. The number of vehicles is not a parameter of the design, so
the same system operates unchanged with a larger or a smaller team.

The system performs online planning. No coverage path, visiting order or list of
targets is prepared before take-off, since none of these can be established for a
building that has not yet been described. Task allocation is dynamic: the share
of the inspection each vehicle is responsible for is determined during the flight
and revised continuously as the state of the search evolves. Mapping is likewise
online, and the resulting map is shared: the vehicles jointly construct a single
three-dimensional description of the building, which is volumetric rather than a
floor plan, as racks have several levels and a code is defined by a height as
much as by a position. Coordination between vehicles is implicit and passes
entirely through this shared description; no communication protocol, negotiation
or consensus mechanism is required.

The system is fault-tolerant, and contains no component whose function is to
detect the failure of a vehicle. It is adaptive to change, whether the contents
of the warehouse have been modified since an earlier mission or an obstacle
appears during the current one. It is self-terminating: the end of the mission is
determined by the team from the state of the inspection, not by a predefined time
budget. Its decisions are semantically guided, in that a pre-trained
vision-language model participates in them, and this guidance is optional---its
removal leaves a system that remains fully operational. Finally, the system is
physically realistic: the vehicles are flown by real autopilots, so that every
decision it produces is one that can be executed in flight.

The system is made of seven elements. They appear in
\Cref{fig:sys:architecture} and are described one by one in the subsections that
follow; the way they run together over a mission is the subject of
\Cref{sec:sys:loop}.

\section{Detailed Architecture Design}%
\label{sec:sys:design}

\subsection{Perception}%
\label{subsec:sys:perception}

Perception is the part of the system that observes the warehouse and provides the
information used by all other components. At each cycle, for each vehicle, it
receives the images from the left and right cameras, their corresponding poses,
and one full \gls{abb:lidar} revolution. It produces three types of information
and writes them into the shared map $\mathcal{M}$: decoded identifiers with their
position and panel orientation, detected but undecoded labels with their position
and approach direction, and regions identified as free or occupied.

The perception function can therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathcal{P}} :
    \mathcal{I}^2 \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathcal{L}
    \longrightarrow
    \mathcal{R} \times \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{V}
    \label{eq:sys:perception}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{I}$ denotes the camera images, $\mathcal{T}$ the corresponding
camera poses, $\mathcal{L}$ the \gls{abb:lidar} measurements, and $\mathcal{R}$,
$\mathcal{S}$, and $\mathcal{V}$ the decoded labels, spotted labels, and spatial
information respectively.

These three types of information do not have the same cost when they are wrong. A
wrong identifier is permanent because the system does not return to a label once
it has been declared read. A false detection instead causes one unnecessary
flight, while a missed uncertain detection costs nothing because the area will be
observed again later. The perception component therefore favours rejection over
guessing: when an observation is uncertain, it remains silent.

\subsubsection*{The Reading Envelope}

A \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code becomes unreadable before it becomes invisible.
Reading mainly depends on two factors: distance and viewing angle. These factors
can be combined into a single quantity. If a square panel is viewed with an angle
$\alpha$, its apparent size is reduced by $\cos\alpha$, which is equivalent to
observing the same panel from a distance $1/\cos\alpha$ times larger. We
therefore define the apparent distance as

\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{app}} = \frac{d}{\cos\alpha},
    \label{eq:sys:apparent}
\end{equation}

where $d$ is the actual distance to the panel and $\alpha$ is the angle between
the camera viewing direction and the panel normal.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_distance_apparente}
    \caption[Distance and angle produce the same effect.]{Distance and angle
        produce the same effect on the apparent size of the \glsxtrshort{abb:qr}
        code. A panel tilted by $\alpha$ is equivalent to an untilted panel at
        $1/\cos\alpha$ times the distance.}%
    \label{fig:sys:apparent-distance}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

dependence on this apparent distance.

\FloatBarrier

These measurements define the reading envelope

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}
    =
    \left\{
    (d,\alpha)\;:\;
    1.5 \leq d_{\text{app}} \leq 4.0
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:sys:envelope}
\end{equation}

Thus, $\mathcal{E}$ is the range of distance-and-angle conditions in which
\glsxtrshort{abb:qr} reading is considered reliable.

The two limits have different causes. The upper limit is optical: beyond about
four metres, the \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} modules occupy too few pixels. The lower
limit is mechanical: at very short distances, small lateral errors produce a
large viewing angle, making it difficult for the vehicle to remain correctly
aligned. The warehouse geometry also matters. Since the main aisle is 3.9\,m
wide, a vehicle cannot simply move farther away from a panel to read several
cartons. Coverage must therefore rely on suitable viewing angles rather than
increasing distance.

The reading envelope is consequently the main constraint used later when
generating \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} reading positions.

remain detectable after it has become unreadable. This distinction allows a
vehicle to detect labels while moving along an aisle and return later to read
them from a suitable position.

only a suitable reading position is still required.

\subsubsection*{Three-Dimensional Placement of an Observation}

An image provides the direction of an observation but not its distance. Three
possible sources can provide this distance: the apparent size of the
\glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code, the translation obtained from solving its pose, or the
\gls{abb:lidar}.

The first two approaches require the physical size of the code. In the simulated
warehouse, each carton carries a square panel of side $s = 0.40$\,m. However, the
panel is larger than the \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code itself because the code
contains a two-module empty margin on each side of its 21-module grid. Since the
decoder returns the \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} corners rather than the panel corners,
the actual \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} side length is

\begin{equation}
    c = s \cdot \frac{21}{25} = 0.336\ \text{m}.
    \label{eq:sys:codeside}
\end{equation}

Using $s$ instead of $c$ would overestimate the distance by about 19\%.

When the four \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} corners are available, the PnP solution
provides both the code orientation and its translation. The orientation is
independent of the assumed physical size, whereas the translation depends
directly on that size. The system therefore uses the corners to obtain
orientation but uses \gls{abb:lidar} to determine distance.

For an image point $\mathbf{u}$, the camera calibration matrix $K$ converts the
pixel into a viewing ray. The ray is transformed into the world frame using the
camera rotation $R_c$. If $\mathbf{p}_c$ is the camera position and
$\rho(\mathbf{u})$ is the measured \gls{abb:lidar} range along that ray, the
three-dimensional observation position is

\begin{equation}
    \rho(\mathbf{u})
    =
    R_c \,
    \iota
    \left(
    \frac{K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}}
    {\left\lVert K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{u}} \right\rVert}
    \right),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{p}
    =
    \mathbf{p}_c + r\,\rho(\mathbf{u}).
    \label{eq:sys:ray}
\end{equation}

Here, $\tilde{\mathbf{u}}$ is the homogeneous form of the image point, $K$ is the
camera intrinsic matrix, $R_c$ is the camera rotation in the world frame, $r$ is
the \gls{abb:lidar} range, and $\iota(\cdot)$ denotes the transformation used to
express the camera ray in the required frame.

The choice of \gls{abb:lidar} is not mainly motivated by precision, since both
distance estimates agree to within about one centimetre in the tested
environment. It is motivated by independence: the distance estimate does not
depend on the physical format of the printed panel. This is preferable for a
system intended to operate in previously unseen warehouses.

A spotted label is more difficult because the detector provides only an image
region rather than four corners. Its orientation cannot therefore be recovered
directly, and its distance must come from the \gls{abb:lidar}. Because
\gls{abb:lidar} rays have a constant angular spacing, the spacing between
neighbouring rays increases with distance:

\[
    \delta(r) = r\,\Delta\theta,
\]

where $\Delta\theta$ is the angular spacing between adjacent \gls{abb:lidar}
rays.

At eight metres, this spacing becomes larger than one map cell. Beyond this
distance, the closest \gls{abb:lidar} ray may hit a neighbouring carton, a gap in
the rack, or the wall behind the detected label. The system therefore uses two
regimes. Within eight metres, the \gls{abb:lidar} provides the distance directly.
Beyond eight metres, the system uses the shared map and selects the first
occupied cell along the detected direction.

    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_regimes}
    \caption[Two placement regimes.]{Two placement regimes. Within the trusted
        \gls{abb:lidar} range, the ray directly provides the label distance.

\FloatBarrier

A final consistency check uses the detected region width. If $w$ is the width of
the detected region in pixels and $f_x$ is the camera focal length in pixels, an
approximate distance is

\[
    \hat{d} = \frac{f_x c}{w}.
\]

This estimate is only used as a plausibility check because the detected region is
not exactly the \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code and may be partially occluded. A
placement that differs from $\hat{d}$ by more than a wide tolerance is rejected.
This mainly prevents a \gls{abb:lidar} ray from passing through a rack opening
and placing the label incorrectly behind the rack.

Finally, a spotted label is considered to face the camera that detected it.
Although this does not provide its exact normal, it gives the side from which the
label should later be approached.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{PlaceObservation}%
    \label{algo:sys:place}
    \KwRequire{$K$, $(\mathbf{p}_c, R_c)$, cloud $\mathcal{C}$, map $\mathcal{M}$,
        and either corners $Q$ (decoded) or a region $B$ (detected)}
    \KwEnsure{$(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{n}) \in \reals^3 \times \reals^3$, or $\bot$}
    \eIf{$Q$ is given}{
        $(R_q, \mathbf{t}_q) \gets \textsc{PnP}(Q, c, K)$ \tcp*[r]{decoded code}
        $\alpha \gets \angle(-\mathbf{t}_q,\, R_q \mathbf{e}_z)$\;
        \lIf{$\alpha > \alpha_{\max}$}{\KwRet$\bot$}
        $\mathbf{u} \gets \textsc{centroid}(Q)$\;
    }{
        $\mathbf{u} \gets \textsc{centre}(B)$\;
    }
    $\rho \gets R_c \cdot \iota\!\left( K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{u}} \,/\, \lVert K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{u}} \rVert \right)$ \tcp*[r]{world ray, \Cref{eq:sys:ray}}
    $r \gets \textsc{RangeLidar}(\mathbf{p}_c, \rho, \mathcal{C})$\;
    \If{$r = \bot$ or $r > r_{\max}$}{
        \lIf{$Q$ is given}{\KwRet$\bot$ \tcp*[f]{a read code is always close}}
        $r \gets \textsc{RangeMap}(\mathbf{p}_c, \rho, \mathcal{M})$ \tcp*[r]{first occupied cell}
    }
    \lIf{$r = \bot$}{\KwRet$\bot$}
    $\mathbf{p} \gets \mathbf{p}_c + r\,\rho$\;
    \eIf{$Q$ is given}{
        \lIf{$r \notin \mathcal{E}$}{\KwRet$\bot$ \tcp*[f]{reading envelope, \Cref{eq:sys:envelope}}}
        $\mathbf{n} \gets R_c \cdot \iota(R_q \mathbf{e}_z)$, oriented towards $\mathbf{p}_c$\;
    }{
        $\hat{d} \gets f_x \, c \,/\, w(B)$\;
        \lIf{$\lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_c \rVert \notin [\kappa_{lo}\hat{d},\, \kappa_{hi}\hat{d}]$}{\KwRet$\bot$}
        $\mathbf{n} \gets -\Pi_{xy}\,\rho \,/\, \lVert \Pi_{xy}\,\rho \rVert$ \tcp*[r]{direction of approach}
    }
    \KwRet$(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{n})$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection*{Why Detection Must Be Learned}

Extending the detection range is not difficult by itself. A geometric detector
based on thresholds and shape tests can detect panels beyond the reading
envelope, but it produces too many false positives. In scenes without
\glsxtrshort{abb:qr} codes, such a detector reports panels in more than a quarter
of the images by confusing cartons, rack structures, and shadows with labels.

Since every spotted label can generate a flight, such a false-positive rate would
produce many unnecessary missions. The detector must therefore satisfy two
conditions simultaneously.

\FloatBarrier

A simple geometric threshold cannot satisfy both requirements: increasing the
threshold reduces false detections but also removes distant true labels. The two
objectives must therefore be separated by a learned detector that recognises the
appearance of labels in the warehouse setting rather than relying only on
geometric shape.

The detector is trained without manual annotation. Because the positions of
cartons and panels are known in simulation, their image regions can be generated
by projecting them using the exact camera pose. Visibility is additionally
checked by casting rays towards points on each surface and keeping only points
for which the corresponding object is the first surface reached. The buildings
used to generate training examples are kept separate from those used for
evaluation.

The detector also identifies cartons as a second class. Cartons are not read, but
their detection provides semantic information for the map, allowing the system to
distinguish occupied rack regions from empty ones before any identifier has been
decoded.

\subsubsection*{The Perception Cycle}

The vehicle uses two lateral cameras rather than a single forward camera because
labels are located on both sides of the aisles. A vehicle flying near the aisle
centre can therefore observe both racks during one pass. With a forward camera,
it would need to turn towards each rack separately, increasing the number of
required reading positions.

Decoding and detection do not have the same computational cost. The adopted
strategy is therefore asymmetric: decoding runs on both camera images at every
cycle, while detection runs on only one camera and alternates between the two.
Missing a detection for one cycle is inexpensive because the vehicle will
continue through the area. Missing a decoding opportunity while the vehicle is
correctly positioned in front of a label, however, may require another visit.

Decoding is also applied to the complete image rather than only to detector
proposals. This keeps the two processes independent: even if the learned detector
misses a label, the decoder can still read it when the \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code
is visible.

A final correction accounts for the renderer's one-step delay. Each image is
therefore associated with the pose and \gls{abb:lidar} cloud from the previous
instant rather than the instant at which the image arrives. Without this
correction, the same label observed while approaching and leaving could be placed
at two different positions in the map.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{PerceptionCycle}%
    \label{algo:sys:cycle}
    \KwRequire{vehicle $i$, cycle index $k$, map $\mathcal{M}$, history $H$ (length 2)}
    \KwEnsure{$\mathcal{M}$ updated}
    $(D, r) \gets \textsc{Lidar}(i)$\;
    $\mathcal{M}.\textsc{IntegrateOccupancy}(\mathbf{p}_i, D, r)$\;
    $\mathcal{M}.\textsc{Announce}(i, \mathbf{p}_i)$\;
    push (poses of $L$ and $R$, cloud $\mathcal{C}$) onto $H$\;
    \lIf{$\lvert H \rvert < 2$}{\KwRet\tcp*[f]{one frame of render lag}}
    $(\mathbf{p}_c, R_c, \mathcal{C}) \gets H[t-1]$\;
    \For{$\mathrm{cam} \in \{L, R\}$}{
        $I \gets \textsc{Image}(\mathrm{cam})$\;
        \For{$(\ell, Q) \in \textsc{Decode}(I)$}{
            \lIf{$\ell \notin \mathcal{F}$}{\Continue \tcp*[f]{foreign identifier}}
            $o \gets \textsc{PlaceObservation}(Q, K, \mathbf{p}_c, R_c, \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{M})$\;
            \lIf{$o \neq \bot$}{$\mathcal{M}.\textsc{WriteRead}(\ell, o)$}
        }
        \If{$\mathrm{cam} \equiv k \pmod 2$}{
            \For{$(\kappa, B) \in \textsc{Detect}(I)$}{
                $o \gets \textsc{PlaceObservation}(B, K, \mathbf{p}_c, R_c, \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{M})$\;
                \lIf{$o = \bot$}{\Continue}
                \lIf{$\kappa = \mathrm{qr}$}{$\mathcal{M}.\textsc{WriteSpotted}(o)$}
                \lElse{$\mathcal{M}.\textsc{MarkSemantic}(o.\mathbf{p})$}
            }
        }
        $\mathcal{M}.\textsc{IntegrateCoverage}(\mathbf{p}_c, R_c \mathbf{e}_x)$\;
    }
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The Shared Map}%
\label{subsec:sys:map}

The shared map is the common memory of the team. It is maintained as a single
instance: the vehicles do not keep separate maps that must later be merged, but
directly read from and write to the same structure. It is also the only
communication channel between the vehicles; no direct messages are exchanged.

The map answers four questions required by the mission: which parts of the space
are occupied or free, which surfaces have already been observed from a readable
direction, which labels exist and which of them have been decoded, and where the
teammates and their current claims are located.

The complete structure occupies about 1.5\,MB for 258,048 cells. It is therefore
updated several times per second and read by every vehicle at each decision
without becoming a computational constraint.

\subsubsection*{Two Structures Rather Than One}

The spatial information is stored in a cubic grid with a resolution of 0.25\,m. A
cell is accessed directly from its spatial coordinates, without any search, which
makes the grid suitable for repeated spatial queries and for \gls{abb:lidar}
integration.

Labels are stored separately in a table. Their positions are maintained with
about 1\,cm precision, which a 0.25\,m cell would discard, and several labels may
occupy the same cell. Each entry stores the decoded code, its position, its
facing direction, and the number of readings used to estimate it.

\subsubsection*{Occupancy by Accumulated Evidence}

For each \gls{abb:lidar} ray, the space crossed by the ray is considered free,
while the cell where the ray stops is considered occupied when an obstacle is
detected. Instead of storing only the most recent measurement, the map
accumulates evidence over time.

For each cell $c$, $L(c)$ is an accumulated occupancy value. A free measurement
adds $\ell_{\mathrm{free}}$, which is negative, and an occupied measurement adds
$\ell_{\mathrm{solid}}$, which is positive. The value is bounded by
$\operatorname{clip}$ to prevent unlimited accumulation. The cell state is then
determined by two thresholds, $\tau_{+}$ and $\tau_{-}$:

\begin{equation}
    L(c) \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}\bigl(L(c)+\ell\bigr),
    \qquad
    \operatorname{state}(c)=
    \begin{cases}
        \mathrm{occupied}, & L(c)>\tau_{+},   \\[2pt]
        \mathrm{free},     & L(c)<\tau_{-},   \\[2pt]
        \mathrm{unknown},  & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:sys:occupancy}
\end{equation}

Here, $\ell$ is either $\ell_{\mathrm{solid}}$ or $\ell_{\mathrm{free}}$,
depending on the \gls{abb:lidar} measurement, and $\operatorname{clip}$ limits
$L(c)$ to the allowed range so that new observations can still revise the state
of a cell.

This accumulation makes the map less sensitive to isolated measurements. For
example, a cell crossed as free ten times and then measured as occupied once
remains free. A single reflection, grazing measurement, or temporary vehicle
therefore does not turn a free aisle into an obstacle.

Free evidence is deposited at several points along each crossed cell, because a
ray that only grazes a cell corner could otherwise leave that cell marked as
unknown even though the surrounding space is free. The sampling stops one cell
before the impact point so that the obstacle cell itself is not marked as free.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{IntegrateRevolution}%
    \label{algo:sys:integrate}
    \KwRequire{origin $\mathbf{o}$, unit directions $\mathbf{d}_i$, ranges $r_i$, grid $L$}
    \KwEnsure{$L$ updated}
    \For{each ray $i$}{
        $r \gets \min(r_i,\, r_{\max})$ \tcp*[r]{only the trusted part is used}
        \For{each sampled point along $\mathbf{d}_i$ up to $r$ minus one cell}{
            add $\ell_{\mathrm{free}}$ to the corresponding cell\;
        }
        \If{$r_i$ is finite and $r_i \le r_{\max}$}{
            add $\ell_{\mathrm{solid}}$ to the impact cell\;
        }
    }
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection*{Oriented Coverage}

Seeing a rack does not necessarily mean that a \glsxtrshort{abb:qr} code placed
on it could be read. A panel is readable only from the side it faces, so a cell
observed from the far end of an aisle or from behind a rack cannot be considered
covered. The map therefore records whether a code located in a cell and facing a
given direction would have been readable from the observed side.

Each cell stores four bits corresponding to the four horizontal sides. A bit is
set when that side has been observed with an apparent distance inside the reading
envelope $\mathcal{E}$ of \Cref{eq:sys:envelope}, and when no known obstacle
blocks the observation.

Let $C(c)$ denote the four coverage bits of cell $c$, and let
$\kappa(\mathbf{v}) \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ return the index of the horizontal side
closest to the direction $\mathbf{v}$. For a panel with normal vector
$\mathbf{n}$, the corresponding side is considered covered when

\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{covered}(c,\mathbf{n})
    =
    \bigl(C(c)\ \&\ 2^{\kappa(-\mathbf{n})}\bigr)\neq 0 .
    \label{eq:sys:coverage}
\end{equation}

Here, $\&$ denotes the bitwise conjunction. The direction $-\mathbf{n}$ is used
because the panel must be observed from the side towards which it faces.

This distinction prevents a rack from being considered complete simply because a
vehicle has passed near it. Candidate generation can instead determine which
surfaces still require a visit, without knowing whether a label is actually
present there.

\subsubsection*{Label Table}

The label table contains two types of entries: a \emph{panel} for a decoded
label, and a \emph{track} for a detected label that has not yet been decoded.

A panel stores the decoded code, its position, its facing direction, and the
number of readings. When the same panel is read several times, the individual
position estimates are combined to obtain a more stable stored position.

A track stores an estimated position and an observation direction. Repeated
detections are merged when they are sufficiently close, and their position and
direction estimates are averaged. As a vehicle moves along an aisle, the same
label may first be detected from a sharp angle and later from a more frontal
view, so the averaged direction approaches the actual panel normal.

Each carton carries the same code on two opposite faces. Two readings with the
same identifier but sufficiently distant positions may therefore correspond to
two different faces of the same carton, and at most two faces are stored for one
identifier.

Let $\varepsilon$ be the distance threshold below which two readings are
considered to belong to the same face, $p$ the position of a new reading, and
$\operatorname{faces}(\ell)$ the set of faces already stored for identifier
$\ell$. The nearest existing face is updated when it lies within $\varepsilon$;
otherwise a second face is created if fewer than two faces exist; otherwise the
reading is rejected as a decoding error:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
        \text{update the nearest face}, &
        \min\limits_{f\in\operatorname{faces}(\ell)} d(p,f)\leq\varepsilon,                \\[6pt]
        \text{create a second face},    &
        \min\limits_{f\in\operatorname{faces}(\ell)} d(p,f)>\varepsilon
        \ \land\ \lvert\operatorname{faces}(\ell)\rvert<2,                                 \\[4pt]
        \text{reject},                  & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:sys:faces}
\end{equation}

Here, $d(p,f)$ is the distance from the new reading to an existing face. In the
implementation, $\varepsilon = 0.45$\,m, slightly above the carton thickness.

When a label is successfully decoded, nearby uncertain tracks are no longer
needed. Any track located within $\varepsilon$ of the new panel is therefore
removed, which prevents the number of tracks from growing indefinitely during a
long mission.

\subsubsection*{Routes and the Cost of the Unknown}

The map provides the current knowledge of the warehouse structure, and route
planning is performed directly on this representation. A horizontal slice around
the vehicle altitude is used, and each cell receives a traversal cost:

\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{cost}(c)=
    \begin{cases}
        \infty, & \text{known obstacle}, \\[2pt]
        1,      & \text{known free},     \\[2pt]
        K_u,    & \text{unobserved}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:sys:cost}
\end{equation}

Here, $K_u$ is the cost of crossing an unobserved cell, expressed relative to the
cost of a known free cell. In the implementation, $K_u = 20$.

The obstacle cells are first dilated by the vehicle radius. Clearance is
therefore handled during route generation rather than during flight, and a route
that exists on this grid can be followed as a sequence of straight segments. A
value of 0.6\,m is used rather than a rounded 0.75\,m radius, since the latter can
close narrow aisles in the simulated warehouse.

Unknown cells are expensive but not forbidden, and both extremes would fail. If
unknown cells were forbidden, a vehicle could never leave the region it has
already explored. If they were free, the planner would cross racks that have not
yet been observed, since those racks do not appear on the map. With $K_u = 20$,
the planner prefers a route through known free space and enters unknown space
only when this is necessary to reach the target.

The altitude slice extends from 2.0\,m below the vehicle to 0.85\,m above it.
This asymmetric range avoids selecting routes that fly over the racks in the
simulation.

Teammates are also inserted into the same cost grid as moving obstacles, so
vehicles avoid one another during route computation rather than through a late
manoeuvre. If a newly observed obstacle intersects the current route, the route
is checked again and recomputed when necessary.

    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig_cout_inconnu}
    \caption{Route cost associated with known and unknown space.}%
    \label{fig:sys:unknown-cost}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{Route}%
    \label{algo:sys:route}
    \KwRequire{start $a$, goal $b$, altitude $z$, occupancy grid}
    \KwEnsure{a list of waypoints, or $\bot$ if no route exists}
    build $\textsc{CostView}(z - 2.0,\; z + 0.85)$ \tcp*[r]{\Cref{eq:sys:cost}}
    add teammate positions as moving obstacles\;
    \lIf{$b$ is unreachable}{\KwRet$\bot$}
    \If{the straight line from $a$ to $b$ contains only known free space}{
        \KwRet an empty waypoint list\;
    }

    \lIf{no path exists}{\KwRet$\bot$}
    simplify the resulting path\;
    \KwRet the waypoint list\;
\end{algorithm}

its currently claimed target. This information is allowed to expire so that
outdated positions and claims are not kept indefinitely. Claim assignment and

\subsection{Candidate Generation}%
\label{sec:sys:candidates}

Candidate generation converts the current state of the map into the set of
actions that are worth performing. It is executed for one vehicle but does not
depend on which vehicle: the candidates are identical for the whole team, and
only their scores differ. They are therefore computed once from the shared map
and made available to all vehicles rather than recomputed independently at each
cycle.

Three kinds of work are available at any moment. A label that has been spotted
but not decoded can be read. A surface that is known to be occupied but has never
been observed from a readable direction can be covered. A boundary between the
known and unknown regions can be extended. These three actions come from
different layers of the map, but they are represented in the same form so that
they can later be compared on a single scale.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_candidats}
    \caption{The three families of candidates generated from the same map
        state.}%
    \label{fig:sys:candidates}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

\subsubsection*{One Common Form}

A candidate is not a location to reach but an observation to perform. It
therefore stores both the point being observed and the pose from which it will be
observed. Let $\mathbf{o}$ denote the observed point, $\mathbf{q}$ the vehicle
pose, $\psi$ the heading, $\sigma$ the side from which the observation is made,
and $u$ the utility of the action. A candidate is the tuple

\begin{equation}
    \gamma =
    \bigl(\text{kind},\ \mathbf{o},\ \mathbf{q},\ \psi,\ \sigma,\ u\bigr),
    \qquad
    \text{kind} \in \{\textsc{Read},\textsc{Cover},\textsc{Explore}\}.
    \label{eq:sys:candidate}
\end{equation}

Here, $\sigma \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ is one of the four horizontal sides defined in
\Cref{eq:sys:coverage}, and the heading $\psi$ is derived from the observation
direction. Since the reading cameras are lateral, a camera pointing towards
$\mathbf{o}$ requires a heading rotated by ninety degrees from that direction.
For an exploration candidate, no particular direction is required, so the heading
is left unconstrained.

This common form allows the three kinds of work to compete directly in
\Cref{sec:sys:scoring}. Candidate generation therefore defines what can be done
and how useful the observation is expected to be, while the cost of reaching it
is handled separately.

\subsubsection*{Choosing the Observation Pose}

Two candidate families require the vehicle to face a surface. The pose must
therefore be placed on the correct side, at a readable distance, and in space
that the vehicle can actually occupy.

The correct side cannot be taken from the direction in which the label was first
detected. A code observed at seventy degrees from its normal is still read from
the front, not from that angle. The side is instead derived from the structure
carrying the panel. A panel is attached to the long face of a rack, so its normal
is perpendicular to the main axis of the occupied structure surrounding it. That
axis is obtained from the occupied cells in a neighbourhood of the label. Among
the two perpendicular directions, the one where free space is nearest is
retained, while the detection direction is used only to break ties. When too
little structure is present around the label to determine an axis reliably, the
detection direction is used instead.

Once the side $\mathbf{n}$ is known, the pose is placed in front of the surface at
a reading distance. The nominal distance is 2.0\,m, at the centre of the reading
envelope $\mathcal{E}$. If that pose is not usable, closer distances are tried
down to 1.5\,m, then larger ones up to 4.0\,m. A pose is usable when the vehicle
can occupy it and when no known obstacle lies between it and the observed
surface.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{ObservationPose}%
    \label{algo:sys:obspose}
    \KwRequire{observed point $\mathbf{o}$, side normal $\mathbf{n}$, ordered distances $D$, map $\mathcal{M}$}
    \KwEnsure{a pose $\mathbf{q}$, or $\bot$}
    \For{each $d$ in $D$}{
        $\mathbf{q} \gets \mathbf{o} + d\,\mathbf{n} + \text{camera offset}$\;
        clamp the altitude of $\mathbf{q}$ to the flight band\;
        \lIf{$\mathbf{q}$ is not occupiable by the vehicle}{\Continue}
        \lIf{a known obstacle lies between $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{o}$}{\Continue}
        \KwRet$\mathbf{q}$\;
    }
    \KwRet$\bot$\;
\end{algorithm}

\FloatBarrier

Occupiability is evaluated with a clearance of 0.9\,m rather than the 0.6\,m used
for routes. A vehicle holding a pose can oscillate around it, so an observation
position requires more clearance from obstacles than a position that is only
crossed in transit.

All candidates are restricted to a flight band extending from 0.9\,m to 4.5\,m,
and an observation pose is never placed below 1.4\,m. The upper bound is not a
limit of the vehicle but of the task: above 4.5\,m a vehicle flies over the racks
instead of along them, and the panels located near 5\,m belong to the warehouse
signage rather than to the inventory.

\subsubsection*{The Three Families and Their Utilities}

\textbf{Reading targets}: Each undecoded track produces one candidate, provided it
has not been blacklisted and lies below the top shelf level. A track above that
height is treated as a spurious detection rather than a label. The reading pose
is placed as described above, with the side imposed by the surrounding structure.

Repeated failures are handled by the generator itself so that a track that cannot
be read is not proposed indefinitely. The first visit uses the nominal distance
set. If no code is decoded, the second visit uses a closer set centred on
1.2\,m, because small labels may not be readable from two metres. If that also
fails, the third visit uses the opposite side. After three unsuccessful visits,
the track is blacklisted and is no longer generated.

\textbf{Coverage targets}: A coverage target is an occupied cell, within the
flight band, whose free neighbour on a given side has never had the corresponding
coverage bit set. Individual cells are too small to justify a separate flight, so
they are grouped by one metre horizontally and half a metre vertically. A group
containing fewer than three cells is discarded as noise. The observed point is
the centre of the group, and the pose is placed on the side that defines it.

\textbf{Exploration targets}: Frontier cells are free cells that touch the
unknown region, taken within the same flight band. An isolated unknown cell
surrounded by known space is not treated as a frontier, since it is a sampling
gap rather than an unexplored area; the unknown must form a connected region for
the frontier to be generated. Frontier cells are grouped by two metres, and the
pose is placed at the centre of the group, at a fixed exploration altitude,
without any imposed heading.

The utility of a candidate depends only on what it is expected to return, not on
the cost of reaching it, which belongs to \Cref{sec:sys:scoring}. For a coverage
group $G$ and a frontier group $F$:

\begin{equation}
    u_{\text{\textsc{Cover}}}(G)
    =
    \min\left(
    \sum_{c \in G} \omega(c),\
    U_{\max}^{\text{cov}}
    \right),
    \qquad
    u_{\text{\textsc{Explore}}}(F)
    =
    \min\left(
    \omega_f \cdot \lvert F \rvert,\
    U_{\max}^{\text{exp}}
    \right)
    \label{eq:sys:utility}
\end{equation}

where $\omega(c)$ is the weight of one surface cell, $\omega_f$ the weight of one
frontier cell, and $U_{\max}^{\text{cov}}$, $U_{\max}^{\text{exp}}$ the two caps.
A surface cell normally has weight 1, but its weight is increased to 3 when the
semantic layer indicates that the cell contains a carton.

    \centering
    \caption{Utility values and their origin.}%
    \label{tab:sys:utilities}
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.6cm}
            r
            >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6.4cm}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Quantity}                                    & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Reason}                                  \\
        \midrule
        Utility of a reading target                          & 30             & A spotted label is an almost certain identifier  \\
        \addlinespace
        Weight of one surface cell, $\omega(c)$              & 1              & Surface has value through accumulated coverage   \\
        \addlinespace
        Weight of a surface cell containing a carton         & 3              & The semantic layer indicates inventory is present \\
        \addlinespace
        Cap on a coverage group, $U_{\max}^{\text{cov}}$     & 30             & Equal to one reading                             \\
        \addlinespace
        Weight of one frontier cell, $\omega_f$              & 0.15           & A frontier yields knowledge, not identifiers     \\
        \addlinespace
        Cap on an exploration group, $U_{\max}^{\text{exp}}$ & 20             & Below one reading                                \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\FloatBarrier

Two properties follow from these values. A coverage group can at most equal a
single reading, so no accumulation of surface outweighs a label that is already
known to exist; when the two are equal, the travel cost decides between them.
Exploration is capped below both, so the team explores when little else remains
and returns to reading as soon as new labels are spotted. The balance between
exploring and collecting is therefore produced by the values themselves, and no
phase or schedule is required.

Although the three families come from different layers of the map and represent
very different kinds of work, they are expressed as the same tuple and can
therefore be compared without any additional rule.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{GenerateCandidates}%
    \label{algo:sys:generate}
    \KwRequire{map $\mathcal{M}$}
    \KwEnsure{a list of candidates $\Gamma$}
    $\Gamma \gets \emptyset$\;
    \For{each track in $\mathcal{M}$ not blacklisted and below the top shelf}{
        $\mathbf{n} \gets$ side imposed by the surrounding structure\;
        $D \gets$ distance set for the current attempt count\;
        $\mathbf{q} \gets \textsc{ObservationPose}(\text{track position}, \mathbf{n}, D, \mathcal{M})$\;
        \lIf{$\mathbf{q} \neq \bot$}{add a \textsc{Read} candidate with utility $U_{\text{read}}$}
    }
    \For{each side $\sigma$}{
        collect occupied cells whose free neighbour on $\sigma$ is uncovered\;
        group them by 1\,m horizontally and 0.5\,m vertically\;
        \For{each group of at least 3 cells}{
            $\mathbf{q} \gets \textsc{ObservationPose}(\text{group centre}, \sigma, D, \mathcal{M})$\;
            \lIf{$\mathbf{q} \neq \bot$}{add a \textsc{Cover} candidate with utility from \Cref{eq:sys:utility}}
        }
    }
    group the frontier cells by 2\,m\;
    \For{each group}{
        $\mathbf{q} \gets$ nearest occupiable pose at the exploration altitude\;
        \lIf{$\mathbf{q} \neq \bot$}{add an \textsc{Explore} candidate with utility from \Cref{eq:sys:utility}}
    }
    \KwRet$\Gamma$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Target Selection}%
\label{sec:sys:scoring}

Candidate generation produces the same list for the whole team. Target selection
is where the vehicles differ: each vehicle evaluates this list from its own
position and selects one candidate. The selection process is therefore executed
independently on every vehicle, while the shared map is the only common input.

Selection has two main goals. First, it must combine the expected utility of a
candidate with the effort required to reach it. This allows the system to
compare, for example, a valuable target that is far away with a less valuable
target that is nearby. Second, it must make sure that the selected candidate is
reachable. A target that cannot be reached should not be selected.

\subsubsection*{The Score}

Let $\gamma$ be a candidate with utility $u(\gamma)$ as defined in
\Cref{sec:sys:candidates}. Let $\mathbf{p}$ be the current position of the
vehicle, and let $L(\mathbf{p},\gamma)$ be the length of the route from
$\mathbf{p}$ to the observation pose of $\gamma$. The travel cost per metre is
$c_m$. The term $P_r$ is the penalty applied when another vehicle has already
claimed the candidate, while $P_v$ is the penalty applied for each teammate
flying near it. Finally, $\lambda$ controls the influence of the semantic guide,
and $\beta(\gamma)$ is the bonus provided by the guide.

The score of a candidate is

\begin{equation}
    s(\gamma)
    =
    u(\gamma)
    - c_m L(\mathbf{p},\gamma)
    - P_r\,\mathbbm{1}\!\left[\gamma \text{ is claimed}\right]
    - P_v\,n_v(\gamma)
    + \lambda\,\beta(\gamma),
    \label{eq:sys:score}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function and $n_v(\gamma)$ is the
number of teammates currently flying within the neighbour radius of the
candidate.

The claim and neighbour terms are evaluated at the observation pose of the
candidate, whereas the guide bonus is evaluated at its observed point. Both
distances are measured horizontally, so two vehicles at different shelf levels
above one another are still treated as neighbours.

The travel cost is set to $c_m = 1$. This defines the unit of the score: one
point of utility corresponds to one metre of travel. A reading target with a
utility of 30 is therefore worth travelling up to thirty metres for, before the
other penalties and bonuses are considered. The same applies to a coverage group

\subsubsection*{Keeping Vehicles Apart}

Two terms in the score are used to keep the vehicles separated. They act at
different levels.

The claim penalty applies when another vehicle has already announced that it is
going to a candidate. Its value is large enough to remove that candidate from
consideration in normal situations, but it is still implemented as a penalty
rather than as a hard constraint. If all remaining candidates have already been
claimed, the vehicle can still select one. This avoids leaving a vehicle idle
simply because no unclaimed target remains.

The neighbour penalty is different. It applies when a candidate is close to a
teammate, even if that candidate has not been claimed. Its value is 40, which is
higher than the utility of a reading target. A target located within four metres
of another vehicle therefore receives a negative contribution and will normally
lose to an alternative target. The penalty is applied once for each nearby
teammate, so a candidate close to two vehicles receives the penalty twice. In
this way, the vehicles naturally spread through the warehouse without requiring
the space to be explicitly divided between them.

The guide bonus is the only optional term in the score. It is added when the
observed point is inside the area recommended by the semantic guide. An
additional bonus is given when the approach side also matches the recommendation.
Exploration candidates receive the zone bonus but not the side bonus because they
do not have a required approach direction. The weight $\lambda$ controls how
strongly the guide affects the ranking. Setting $\lambda = 0$ removes the guide
completely and leaves a purely geometric score. The semantic guide itself is
described in \Cref{subsec:sys:semantic}.

\subsubsection*{Feasibility and the Two-Stage Evaluation}

\Cref{eq:sys:score} requires the route length $L(\mathbf{p},\gamma)$, which is
computed from the map as described in \Cref{subsec:sys:map}. Computing a complete
route for every candidate would be unnecessarily expensive, because several
hundred candidates are available at each decision.

The system therefore evaluates candidates in two stages.

In the first stage, the true route length is replaced by the straight-line
distance $\lVert \mathbf{q}_{\gamma}-\mathbf{p}\rVert$, where
$\mathbf{q}_{\gamma}$ is the observation pose of $\gamma$. This gives a
provisional score $\bar{s}(\gamma)$. A real route cannot be shorter than the
straight-line distance, so
$L(\mathbf{p},\gamma) \geq \lVert \mathbf{q}_{\gamma}-\mathbf{p}\rVert$ and
therefore

\begin{equation}
    \bar{s}(\gamma) \geq s(\gamma)
    \qquad \text{for every candidate } \gamma.
    \label{eq:sys:bound}
\end{equation}

The provisional score is thus an upper bound on the final score. A candidate with
a low provisional score cannot become better once its real route is computed.
This allows the system to discard most candidates before performing the more
expensive route computation.

In the second stage, only the six candidates with the highest provisional scores
are evaluated with an actual route. The candidate with the highest final score is
then selected. This is an approximation of the exact selection, but the
approximation is controlled by the upper-bound property above: candidates are
discarded using an optimistic estimate of their final score rather than an
underestimated one.

Reachability is checked during the same second stage. If the map contains no
valid route to a candidate, that candidate is discarded instead of simply
receiving a lower score. The system therefore does not commit to an observation
that it cannot reach. The reachability test is based on the map available at that
moment, rather than on a predefined plan of the warehouse.

Two simple filters are applied before scoring. Candidates whose observed point
has already been visited three times without success are removed, as described in
\Cref{sec:sys:candidates}. Candidates whose observed point is blacklisted are
also removed.

It is possible for no candidate to survive these stages, either because the list
is empty or because none of the six best candidates is reachable. The vehicle
then holds its current position, and this situation is recorded. If every vehicle
is simultaneously without a target, the mission is considered complete, as
described in \Cref{sec:sys:mission}.

Selection is not repeated at every control cycle. Once a candidate has been
selected, the vehicle keeps it until the observation has been performed, the
target has been abandoned, or the vehicle has been unable to make progress
towards it. Only then is a new selection made.

Finally, once a candidate has been selected, the vehicle announces it in the
shared map before starting the flight. The announcement, its expiration, and its
release are described in \Cref{sec:sys:claims}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{SelectTarget}%
    \label{algo:sys:select}
    \KwRequire{vehicle position $\mathbf{p}$, candidate list $\Gamma$, map $\mathcal{M}$, guide advice $A$, weight $\lambda$}
    \KwEnsure{the selected candidate, or $\bot$}
    $\Gamma \gets \{\, \gamma \in \Gamma : \gamma$ not blacklisted and visited fewer than 3 times $\}$\;
    \For{each $\gamma \in \Gamma$}{
        $\bar{s}(\gamma) \gets$ score with the straight-line distance \tcp*[r]{\Cref{eq:sys:score,eq:sys:bound}}
    }
    sort $\Gamma$ by decreasing $\bar{s}$\;
    $\mathit{best} \gets \bot$\;
    \For{each $\gamma$ among the six first}{
        $\mathit{route} \gets \textsc{Route}(\mathbf{p}, \text{pose of } \gamma, \text{altitude of } \gamma)$ \tcp*[r]{\Cref{algo:sys:route}}
        \lIf{$\mathit{route} = \bot$}{\Continue \tcp*[f]{unreachable, discarded}}
        $L \gets$ length of the route\;
        $s(\gamma) \gets$ score with $L$ \tcp*[r]{\Cref{eq:sys:score}}
        \lIf{$\mathit{best} = \bot$ or $s(\gamma) > s(\mathit{best})$}{$\mathit{best} \gets \gamma$}
    }
    \KwRet$\mathit{best}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Coordination}%
\label{sec:sys:claims}

Coordination prevents the vehicles from duplicating work and helps them avoid
collisions, without requiring direct communication between them. No message is
exchanged and no agreement is negotiated. Instead, each vehicle writes its
current state into the shared map and reads the information written by the
others. All coordination rules described in this section therefore operate on the
same shared structure.

Separation is handled at three different scales because the problems are
different. Two vehicles may select the same target, which is a task allocation
problem. Two vehicles may plan routes that cross, which is a path planning
problem. Two vehicles may simply get too close during flight, which is an
immediate safety problem. Each case is handled by a different mechanism.

\subsubsection*{Claims and Their Expiry}

When a vehicle selects a target, it writes a claim into the map before starting
to fly. The claim associates the vehicle with the target position and includes an
expiry time. Other vehicles do not treat this claim as a strict prohibition.
Instead, they apply the penalty $P_r$ from \Cref{eq:sys:score} to candidates
located within four metres of the claimed position.

A claim is not permanent. Let $e_i$ be the expiry time of the claim held by
vehicle $i$, $\tau_i$ the last time that vehicle published its position in the
map, and $T_s$ the tolerated silence period. At time $t$, the claim is active
only if

\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{active}(i,t)
    \iff
    \bigl(t < e_i\bigr)
    \ \wedge\
    \bigl(t-\tau_i \leq T_s\bigr).
    \label{eq:sys:claim-active}
\end{equation}

The first condition gives the claim a fixed lifetime of 45\,s, counted from the
moment the target is assigned. The claim is not extended while the vehicle works
on the target; it is simply replaced when the vehicle selects its next one. The
duration must therefore be long enough to cover a complete observation, including
the transit, the approach, and the time spent holding the pose, and short enough
that a vehicle which stops making progress does not reserve a target for much
longer than it can use it.

The second condition removes the claim when the vehicle has stopped reporting its
position, with $T_s = 5$\,s. A vehicle publishes its position at every cycle, so
this condition is satisfied continuously in normal operation.

The same expiry mechanism is used for temporary blacklist entries. A target that
has been abandoned is excluded for 120\,s rather than permanently, because the
reason for the abandonment may disappear. For example, an obstacle may be
removed, or a valid route may become available after more of the building has
been mapped. A track that has failed three reading attempts is treated
differently and is excluded permanently, because repeated failure is considered
evidence that the detection itself was incorrect rather than that the approach
was temporarily difficult.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{AgeSharedState}%
    \label{algo:sys:age}
    \KwRequire{map $\mathcal{M}$, current time $t$}
    \KwEnsure{expired entries removed, silent vehicles reported}
    remove every claim whose expiry time is earlier than $t$\;
    remove every blacklist entry whose expiry time is earlier than $t$\;
    $S \gets \{\, i : t - \tau_i > T_s \,\}$ \tcp*[r]{vehicles no longer reporting}
    \For{each $i \in S$}{
        remove the claim held by $i$\;
    }
    \KwRet$S$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection*{Separation in Space}

Claims prevent two vehicles from selecting the same work, but they do not control
the physical distance between them. Two targets may be four metres apart and
still have routes that cross.

During route planning, teammates are therefore added to the cost grid of
\Cref{subsec:sys:map} as moving obstacles with a radius of 2\,m. A vehicle
computing a route consequently treats the other vehicles as obstacles and tries
to avoid them from the beginning. This is preferable to correcting the trajectory
only after the vehicles have already come close.

During flight, a simpler rule provides a final safety layer. When two vehicles
are closer than 2.5\,m horizontally and their altitude difference is less than
1.2\,m, the vehicle with the higher index stops and gives way to the other. When
the horizontal distance falls below 1.5\,m, both vehicles stop, regardless of
their priority.

The priority based on the vehicle index is arbitrary, but it gives the team a
fixed order. This guarantees that two vehicles facing each other do not both yield
or both continue.

The vertical condition is also important. Vehicles can work on different shelf
levels of the same rack, so a small horizontal distance does not necessarily mean
that they are in conflict. Considering altitude prevents unnecessary stops when
the vehicles are safely separated vertically.

\subsubsection*{Releasing Work}

A claim is released as soon as the vehicle stops working on its target. Four
situations can cause this release, and each one leads to a different state for
the target afterwards.

The second and third rows describe the same mechanism activated by two different
triggers. In both cases the target is placed in an abandoned state, and the
release together with the blacklist entry are applied when the vehicle performs
its next selection.

The third case is important because one simple rule covers several possible
failures. If a vehicle has held a target for fifteen seconds without moving at
least thirty centimetres, it is considered stuck. The cause may be another vehicle
blocking the aisle, a structure that is not yet present in the map, or repeated
route recomputations. The system does not try to identify the exact cause.
Instead, it releases the target, blacklists it temporarily, and starts a new
selection.

The fourth case allows the team to continue after the loss of a vehicle. A
vehicle that stops flying also stops publishing its position in the map, and
\Cref{eq:sys:claim-active} then invalidates its claims once the silence tolerance
is reached. The targets it was holding become available to the other vehicles,
and the mission continues.

This mechanism requires no diagnosis, since the absence of new state information
is sufficient whatever the cause. Two situations are nevertheless detected
explicitly, because they can be recognised without ambiguity and because
releasing the work immediately is preferable to waiting for the silence delay: a
vehicle whose altitude falls below 0.3\,m, and a vehicle whose tilt exceeds 70
degrees. In both cases the vehicle is marked as lost and its claims are released
at once. The silence rule remains the general mechanism and covers every other
case, including a vehicle that has become unresponsive without falling.

\subsection{The Semantic Guide}%
\label{subsec:sys:semantic}

The semantic guide is the only component of the system that does not rely only on
geometric information. It is a pre-trained \gls{abb:vlm} that receives the
current state of the search and suggests where the vehicle should look next. Its
answer does not directly select a target. Instead, it is converted into a bonus
that is added to the score in \Cref{eq:sys:score}, with a weight $\lambda$.

The guide is optional by design. Setting $\lambda = 0$ removes its contribution
from the score, so the system behaves exactly as if the guide were not present.
All other components continue to work normally because none of them depends on
the guide's advice.

\subsubsection*{What the Guide Is Asked}

The guide is not asked an open question about the mission. Such a question would
produce free text that would then have to be interpreted before it could be used.
Instead, the two questions are designed so that each answer can be given with a
single word or number.

The first question asks which area the vehicle should visit next. To make this
possible, the candidates generated in \Cref{sec:sys:candidates} are grouped on a
three-metre grid. The groups are then sorted by their total utility, and at most
six groups are kept and numbered. The guide only needs to return one of these
numbers, so no interpretation of free text is required.

The second question asks from which side the shelf in the selected area should be
approached. The answer is one of the four horizontal sides, expressed as a
cardinal direction. This question is useful because determining the correct
observation side is a recurring difficulty for the geometric part. As described
in \Cref{sec:sys:candidates}, the side is inferred from the structure surrounding
a label, but this inference can be unreliable when the surrounding structure has
not yet been sufficiently mapped.

Each vehicle sends its own request and keeps its own advice. The distances given
in the description are measured from the position of the vehicle that asked, so
two vehicles in different parts of the building do not receive the same answer.

\subsubsection*{What the Guide Is Given}

Two rules define the information given to the guide. Both are deliberate design
choices.

First, the guide receives only information that is already known from the map. It
never receives the position of an unread code, the complete layout of the
building, or any other information that the team has not discovered through
observation. Otherwise, the guide could answer using information that is
unavailable to the vehicles, and its contribution would not reflect the actual
search process.

Second, the guide receives facts, not scores. For each zone, it is given the
number of codes that have been detected but not yet read, the number of shelf
sections containing boxes that have not yet been observed, whether unexplored
space is present, the facing direction of the shelves, and the distance from the
vehicle to the zone. It also receives the number of codes read since the
beginning of the mission. It is not given the utility or score already computed
by the geometric selector. This prevents the guide from simply reproducing the
geometric ranking and allows its advice to provide an additional source of
information.

The state can be presented to the guide in two forms. The first is visual: the
model receives the current camera image together with a top view of the map
showing the candidate zones and their numbers. The second is textual: the same
information is provided as a structured description of each zone, without an
image. Both forms contain the same facts and differ only in how those facts are
presented. This allows models that accept images and models that do not to be
evaluated on the same task.

\subsubsection*{How the Advice Enters the Decision}

The guide returns a zone defined by a centre and a radius, and optionally an
approach side. Let $\mathbf{o}_\gamma$ be the observed point of candidate
$\gamma$, $\sigma_\gamma$ its approach side, $(\mathbf{z},\rho)$ the advised
zone, and $\sigma_a$ the advised side. The bonus used in \Cref{eq:sys:score} is

\begin{equation}
    \beta(\gamma)
    =
    B_z\,
    \mathbbm{1}\!\left[
        \lVert \mathbf{o}_\gamma-\mathbf{z}\rVert \leq \rho
        \right]
    +
    B_s\,
    \mathbbm{1}\!\left[
        \sigma_\gamma=\sigma_a
        \ \wedge\
        \operatorname{kind}(\gamma)\neq\textsc{Explore}
        \right],
    \label{eq:sys:guide-bonus}
\end{equation}

where $B_z = 10$ is the bonus for being inside the advised zone and $B_s = 5$ is
the additional bonus for matching the advised side. Both distances are measured
horizontally. Exploration candidates receive the zone bonus but not the side
bonus because they have no imposed approach direction.

The guide is never allowed to block the vehicle. A request is sent in the
background while the vehicle continues its current task. When the answer becomes
available, it is stored and used from the next decision cycle. A minimum interval
separates two consecutive requests from the same vehicle, and a new request is
sent only after the previous request has finished.

An advice is kept until a new one replaces it. It can therefore be applied to
several successive decisions, and it may become slightly outdated while the map
continues to change. This is acceptable because the advice only adds a bonus: an
outdated suggestion can make a candidate less attractive than it should be, but
it cannot make an unreachable or already completed target be selected.

If no advice has been received, or if the model returns an answer that does not
correspond to any of the proposed zones, $\beta(\gamma)$ is set to zero. The
vehicle then makes its decision using the geometric score alone.

This asynchronous design makes the guide safe to add to the system. The decision
loop never waits for the model. A slow response therefore does not stop the
vehicle, and a missing or invalid response has no effect on the rest of the
system.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
    \caption{RequestAdvice}%
    \label{algo:sys:advice}
    \KwRequire{vehicle $i$, map $\mathcal{M}$, candidate list $\Gamma$, minimum interval $\Delta$}
    \KwEnsure{the advice held by vehicle $i$ is updated when one is available}
    \lIf{the last request from $i$ is more recent than $\Delta$}{\KwRet}
    \If{a request from $i$ is still running}{
        \lIf{it has not completed}{\KwRet}
        $A \gets$ its result\;
        \lIf{$A \neq \bot$}{store $A$ as the advice of vehicle $i$}
    }
    $Z \gets$ group $\Gamma$ on a 3\,m grid, sort by total utility, keep at most 6, number them\;
    \lIf{$Z = \emptyset$}{\KwRet}
    build the state description from $\mathcal{M}$ and $Z$ \tcp*[r]{facts only, no scores}
    send the two questions in the background\;
\end{algorithm}

\section{System Operation}%
\label{sec:sys:mission}%
\label{sec:sys:loop}

The previous sections described six components separately. This section shows how
they run together during a mission. The flight controller that executes a chosen
pose is not described here: it belongs to the vehicle platform rather than to the
contribution of this work, and it is presented in Chapter 4.

\subsection{Three Rhythms}%
\label{subsec:sys:rhythms}

The components do not run at the same rate, and this is what allows a slow
component to be present without slowing the mission.

A cycle lasts 0.2\,s of simulated time. During each cycle, every vehicle that is
still flying integrates its \gls{abb:lidar} revolution into the map, publishes
its position, and decodes both camera images. The learned detector is more
expensive, so it runs for one vehicle per cycle and on one of its two cameras,
alternating. With three vehicles, each one therefore runs the detector once every
three cycles.

At most one vehicle makes a decision per cycle. This is the rule that keeps the
decision out of the control loop: the other vehicles continue to fly on the
target they already hold while one of them chooses its next.

\subsection{One Cycle}%
\label{subsec:sys:cycle}

A cycle follows the same order every time. The map is updated first, so that
every decision taken afterwards uses the most recent state. Expired claims and
blacklist entries are then removed. Vehicles that are flying towards a target
check that their route is still valid, and recompute it if a newly observed
obstacle has cut it. Finally, one vehicle without a current target selects a new
one, announces it in the map, and starts flying.

A vehicle that has reached a reading pose holds it for two seconds, which
corresponds to ten images at the observation rate. A vehicle that has no target
and cannot find one waits two seconds before trying again, so that a temporary
absence of candidates does not consume the decision slot at every cycle.

\subsection{Ending the Mission}%
\label{subsec:sys:termination}

The team decides for itself when the inventory is finished. Four conditions end
the mission, and the first one to occur applies.

The first condition assumes that the size of the inventory is known in advance,

the share is reached, and with an unlimited budget it would keep flying for a
single unreachable label.

The second and third conditions do not use that assumption. They end the mission
when progress has stopped, either because no new identifier is being found or
because no vehicle can find anything left to do.

\subsection{Behaviour When Conditions Change}%
\label{subsec:sys:change}

The three situations that a prepared plan cannot absorb are handled by the
mechanisms already described. None of them is treated as a special case, and no
component exists to detect them.

\textbf{An obstacle appears in an aisle}: The \gls{abb:lidar} measures it, and the
occupancy layer confirms it as solid after a few revolutions. Those cells become
impassable in the cost grid, the route re-check finds that the current route is
cut, and a new route is computed. If no route remains, the target is abandoned
and blacklisted for 120\,s, and the vehicle selects another one.

\textbf{The warehouse has changed since a previous mission}: The map is built from
nothing at every mission, so a building whose contents have moved presents no
difficulty. The same mechanism also works within a mission: because occupancy
accumulates evidence rather than storing the last measurement, an object that is
removed stops receiving occupied evidence and returns to free once enough rays
have crossed it.

\textbf{A vehicle is lost}: It stops publishing its position, its claims expire,
and the work it was holding becomes available to the others, as described in
\Cref{sec:sys:claims}. The remaining vehicles continue with a larger share of the
building each.

\section{Conclusion}%
\label{sec:sys:conclusion}

This chapter presented the design of a system that takes a complete
\glsxtrshort{abb:qr}-code inventory of a warehouse whose layout is not known in
advance. It is built on a single organising decision: the plan is replaced by a
description that the vehicles write themselves while they fly, and every action
the system takes is derived from that description rather than prepared before
take-off. \Cref{sec:sys:problem} established why this was necessary, since no
coverage path, visiting order or target list can be written for a building that
has not yet been described, and \Cref{sec:sys:overview} stated the consequences
that follow from it. Every later section builds on this decision rather than
around it.

The remaining sections turned that decision into a concrete system, and each one
answered a difficulty left open by the previous one.
\Cref{subsec:sys:perception} established what can be known at all, and showed
that detecting a label and reading it are two functions with ranges that differ
by a factor of two; the intermediate state between them is what gives the mission
something to collect. \Cref{subsec:sys:map} organised those observations into
four layers, among which oriented coverage is what makes remaining work definable
without knowing where the labels are. \Cref{sec:sys:candidates} converted those
layers into three kinds of work written in a single form, so that reading,
covering and exploring could be compared at all. \Cref{sec:sys:scoring} priced
them on one scale where one point of utility equals one metre of flight, which
removed the need for any explicit arbitration between exploring and collecting.
\Cref{sec:sys:claims} kept the vehicles apart through the same shared structure,
using claims that expire rather than a protocol, which is also what makes the
loss of a vehicle harmless. \Cref{subsec:sys:semantic} added the only
non-geometric component, an advice that is weighted and removable.
\Cref{sec:sys:mission} showed the whole system running at three different rates
and deciding for itself when the inventory is complete.

The choices made here are testable claims rather than plausible arrangements.
That a system with no pre-planning can complete an inventory in a building it has
never seen. That a single score is sufficient to balance exploration against
collection, with no phase and no schedule. That coordination through expiring
claims keeps several vehicles apart and absorbs the loss of one without any
component observing their health. That the range of the learned detector, rather
than that of the decoder, governs how quickly the inventory is collected. And
that a pre-trained \gls{abb:vlm}, given only what the team has itself observed,
improves the result over pure geometry---a claim that is equally reportable if it
turns out to be false. Chapter 4 describes how this design was implemented, and
Chapter 5 tests each of these claims in turn.
