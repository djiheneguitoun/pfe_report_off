\section{Problem Formulation}%
\label{sec:sys:problem}

\subsection{The Inspection Task}%
\label{subsec:sys:task}

An inventory inspection compares what is physically present on the shelves with
the records held by the \gls{abb:wms}. Keeping these two views consistent is what
makes the operation necessary, since miscounts and misplaced items reduce order
accuracy and raise operating costs~\cite{pore2026drone}. The task takes as input
a bounded space containing stored items, each carrying a visual identifier on its
faces, and returns the list of identifiers actually found, together with the
position at which each was read. Two properties determine whether that list is
usable. It must account for as large a share of the stored items as possible,
since an item that is not counted is an item the records will continue to report
incorrectly. And it must contain no identifier that does not exist in the
warehouse, since a single spurious entry places the whole list in doubt.

\subsection{Operating Conditions}%
\label{subsec:sys:conditions}

The task is carried out under conditions that no design can change. They are
stated here as facts, and the requirements of the following section follow from
them.

\textbf{The space}: No description of the building is available at take-off. The
number of racks, their position and the location of the items are unknown, and
the free volume is bounded by fixed obstacles, racks, columns and pallets left on
the floor, as well as by moving ones, operators and handling
vehicles~\cite{pore2026drone, pore2026uavqr}. A typical warehouse is a large
building whose racks can exceed ten metres in
height~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}, and the spacing between them is tight enough
to force conservative speed planning~\cite{pore2026drone}. The contents of the
space also change while the mission runs: items are deposited and removed during
the operating day, and an aisle that was clear a minute earlier can be
obstructed. Since neither the layout nor its evolution is known beforehand, no
route through the space and no division of that space between vehicles can be
established before the flight.

\textbf{The reading}: A visual identifier is decodable only within a bounded range
of distances, within a bounded viewing angle and below a bounded relative
speed~\cite{dong2024motionblurred, alsulami2026deep}, so a surface can be
observed without its label being read. Lighting and occlusion narrow that range
further: decoding accuracy falls from 98.5\% in a well-lit environment to 85.2\%
in low light and to 78.3\% when the code is partly
hidden~\cite{alsulami2026deep}, and the motion of the platform produces blur that
lowers the recognition rate~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}. The total number of
items present is not known before the mission, and nothing in the environment
signals that the inventory is complete.

\textbf{The fleet}: The endurance of a single vehicle is bounded, and falls below
what the coverage of a working warehouse demands: small \glspl{abb:uav} typically
fly for twenty to thirty minutes~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}, while a complete
manual inventory check consumes more than three days of warehouse
activity~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}. Several vehicles therefore operate in the
same space, which includes aisles wide enough for only one of them at a time.
Using more than one also shortens the mission, since two drones halved the
inspection time of a single one in a warehouse experiment~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. A
vehicle can stop flying at any moment, from a technical failure or a depleted
battery, and without warning~\cite{chandran2024network}. Perception and decision
run on the same limited onboard computer, and the mission depends on both, a
combination identified in the literature as an open
difficulty~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.

\subsection{Requirements and Constraints}%
\label{subsec:sys:requirements}

From these conditions follow seven results that a solution is expected to
produce. They are stated as outcomes that can be verified on a completed mission
rather than as mechanisms, so that they remain independent of any particular
design.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item The inventory is completed without any description of the layout, any
          route, and any division of work being supplied before take-off.
    \item No identifier is reported that does not correspond to an item present
          in the warehouse.
    \item When a vehicle stops flying, the area it was covering is inspected by
          the remaining vehicles before the mission ends.
    \item Two vehicles do not travel to the same objective, so that the capacity
          of the fleet is not spent twice on a single reading.
    \item No vehicle collides with a surface, with an obstacle that appeared
          after take-off, or with another vehicle.
    \item The mission ends at a moment the system determines by itself, without
          an external instruction, and without spending the remaining flight
          budget on a search that no longer produces readings.
    \item The computation performed for each decision, together with the
          perception running alongside it, remains within the onboard budget and
          within the endurance of the platform.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Position of Existing Approaches}%
\label{subsec:sys:position}

No existing system produces these seven results together. Systems designed for
warehouse inspection receive the layout before take-off and compute their
coverage route once, on the ground, leaving the vehicle to execute
it~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, pore2026uavqr, cristiani2020inventory,
    alsulami2026deep, kwon2020robust, guinand2021uavugv}. What matters is not how
that route is computed but when: the decision is made when the system knows the
least it will ever know, and a target appearing later cannot be taken into
account, because the component that produced the plan is no longer
running~\cite{krayani2023goal}. The same design carries two further assumptions.
The environment is treated as immobile, although operators and handling vehicles
move through a working warehouse, and only about 3\% of inventory studies
consider a dynamic one~\cite{pore2026drone}. And cooperation between flying
vehicles is almost absent: where several vehicles are used, the ground robot
carries the drone or places beacons rather than reading any code
itself~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, guinand2021uavugv}.

The methods that could decide during the flight are not designed for this task.
Those that coordinate several vehicles fix the division of labour before
take-off, by a central schedule~\cite{guinand2021uavugv}, a fixed
split~\cite{pore2026uavqr}, or a leader broadcasting
waypoints~\cite{chandran2024network}, and allocation able to adapt in real time
is still described as a future direction rather than a current
practice~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Semantic reasoning appears in two
separate places, and never where it would be needed. On a ground robot working
alone, it does drive exploration, but the mission solves Object Goal Navigation
and ends as soon as one instance of a designated target is
reached~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm, yu2023l3mvn}, and the representation built for
that target cannot be reused for another~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm}. On a drone, it
only interprets an instruction given by a person: the vehicle carries out what it
has been asked to do and never chooses where to explore~\cite{chen2024typefly}.
Semantic reasoning, cooperation and flight therefore never occur together; three
of the four combinations exist, and the missing one is exactly what an inventory
requires.

The two sides therefore never meet: the systems that perform the right task
cannot react, and the systems that can react were not built for it and stop at
the first success. Four of the seven results are ruled out by that alone. An
inventory cannot be completed without a layout when the layout is what the
planner consumes. The area of a vehicle that stops cannot be reassigned when the
assignment was made once. An obstacle appearing after take-off cannot be avoided
by a route fixed before it existed. And a system that ends at the first detection
cannot decide when an inventory is complete. One caveat applies throughout: these
limitations are established almost entirely on simulated missions, since about
45\% of inventory studies are simulation-based against roughly 7\% that report
real experiments~\cite{pore2026drone}.

\section{Overview of the Proposed Approach}%
\label{sec:sys:overview}

The system produces the inventory of a warehouse whose layout is not known in
advance. Three vehicles take off inside the building. They have no map, only the
boundaries of the space they may occupy. From their sensors, they build a
description of the warehouse while they fly, and every decision they take is
derived from that description at the moment it is taken.

The mission runs as a loop. The vehicles observe, the observations update the
description, the description produces a list of things worth doing, each vehicle
picks one and flies there, and what it finds updates the description again. The
loop stops when the inventory stops growing.

Because the description is rewritten continuously instead of being fixed before
take-off, the same loop handles three situations that a pre-computed plan cannot.
When an obstacle appears in an aisle, it is added to the description and the
paths that crossed it are recomputed. When the contents of the warehouse change,
the old information is replaced rather than kept. And when a vehicle stops
flying, the work it was doing returns to the others automatically, as explained
below.

Three properties hold together in this design, and no published work combines
them. Several vehicles cooperate, and the work is redistributed during the flight
rather than fixed before take-off. They operate in a warehouse they do not know,
building their own representation of it as they go. And a pre-trained
vision-language model takes part in the decision itself.

The system has seven elements, described below and shown in
\Cref{fig:sys:architecture}.

\textbf{Perception}: Each vehicle perceives in two stages, because finding a
label and reading it are two different operations.

A \gls{abb:qr} code can only be decoded from close range and within a narrow band
of viewing angles. A vehicle equipped only with a decoder would therefore be
stuck: it would have to be already in front of a label in order to learn that
this position was worth reaching. A second, coarser stage is needed.

That stage is a learned detector. It finds cartons and \gls{abb:qr} codes in the
camera image from several metres away, without trying to read them, and a range
sensor measures the distance in that direction, which places the detection in
space. The decoder then performs the actual reading once the vehicle is close and
correctly oriented.

A label therefore passes through three states: unknown, spotted but not yet read,
and read. The middle state is the useful one, because it is what gives a vehicle
a reason to move towards a specific place.

\textbf{The shared map}: The team holds one map, and only one. Every vehicle
writes into it what its sensors establish, and reads from it before every
decision. No vehicle keeps a private copy.

The map records four things:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item which parts of the volume are solid, which are free, and which are
          still unknown, so that paths can be computed through space known to be
          clear;
    \item which surfaces have been observed, and from which direction, because a
          rack face seen from one side says nothing about a label mounted on the
          other;
    \item the labels found so far, with their position and their state;
    \item where the other vehicles are, and which target each of them has
          announced.
\end{itemize}

Two consequences follow. A rack mapped by one vehicle becomes immediately usable
by the other two, so adding vehicles reduces the cost of the mission instead of
increasing it. And an observation that contradicts an earlier one replaces it,
which is what allows the system to work in a warehouse that changes while the
mission runs.

\textbf{Candidate actions}: The map is never used to compute a route. It is used
to produce a list of \emph{candidate actions}, meaning the things that are worth
doing at this instant. Three kinds are extracted from the map.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Reading} takes a label that has been spotted but not yet
          decoded. It requires occupying a precise position in front of that
          label.
    \item \textbf{Covering} takes a surface that is known to exist but has never
          been observed from an angle allowing a label on it to be read. This is
          the only way to find labels that have not been spotted yet.
    \item \textbf{Exploring} takes a point on the boundary between the known and
          the unknown parts of the map. Such a boundary is called a
          \emph{frontier}, and going to frontiers is the classical way of
          exploring an unknown space~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm}.
\end{itemize}

These three activities are different in nature, and a system that ranks them by a
fixed priority fails as soon as two of them are worth doing at the same time. To
avoid this, each candidate action is written in the same way, whatever its kind:
\textbf{a position to occupy and a direction to look in}. Once written in this
form, a reading, a covering and an exploration are indistinguishable, and can
therefore be compared.

\textbf{A single scale}: Because all candidate actions are written in the same
form, they can be placed on one common scale. Each one receives a numerical
value, built from three terms.

The first term is what the action is expected to return. Reading a label counts
far more than extending the known region by one cell, which keeps the team
focused on producing inventory entries.

The second term is what the action costs. It is measured as the length of a path
through space known to be free, not as a straight-line distance, so that a target
on the far side of a rack is priced at what it really takes to reach.

The third term is a penalty. It is applied when another vehicle has already
announced that action, or is currently flying close to it.

The vehicle then selects the candidate with the highest value among those it can
actually reach, which is checked by computing the path before committing to it.

The important consequence is that reading and exploring compete against each
other directly, on the same scale. The balance between them is not fixed by a
rule written in advance: while labels are waiting to be read, reading wins on its
own, and when there are none left, exploration takes over, without any mode being
switched.

\textbf{Claiming and releasing}: No component of the system assigns work to a
vehicle. Coordination works the other way round.

When a vehicle selects an action, it writes that choice into the shared map. This
is called a \emph{claim}. The other vehicles read the map before their own next
decision, see the claim, and lower the value of every candidate close to it, so
they naturally go elsewhere.

A claim is granted by nobody and negotiated with nobody. It simply expires if the
vehicle does not renew it periodically.

This one rule does two jobs. It prevents two vehicles from travelling to the same
target, which is what makes a team worth having in the first place. And it
absorbs the loss of a vehicle: one that stops flying also stops renewing its
claims, those claims expire after a short delay, and the actions it was holding
return to the common list, where the others pick them up at their next ordinary
decision. No component watches for failures, and none is written to react to one.
Fault tolerance is a side effect of the expiry rule, not a feature added on top of
it.

\textbf{Semantic guidance}: One further element takes part in the choice.

At regular intervals, a vehicle sends a short textual description of the current
map to a pre-trained vision-language model, and asks which area of the warehouse
deserves attention next, and from which side it should be approached. The answer
raises the value of every candidate located in that area. This follows the
principle used in semantic frontier selection, where a pre-trained model
estimates which frontier is most likely to lead somewhere
useful~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm, yu2023l3mvn}.

The reason for adding this element is that geometry alone knows where the team
has \emph{not} looked, but it has no way of knowing that a corner full of cartons
is more promising than an empty aisle. That kind of judgement is precisely what a
model trained on very large quantities of images and text can provide, without
any training specific to this task.

Two choices keep the system independent of the model. The contribution is
multiplied by a weight, and setting that weight to zero removes the model
entirely, leaving a purely geometric system. And the request is sent
asynchronously, so if the answer has not arrived when a decision is due, the
decision is taken without it.

\textbf{Execution}: Once an action has been selected, a controller brings the
vehicle to the required position in three phases. It transits quickly along the
path computed through the known free space. It then slows down for the final
approach, so as to arrive without overshooting. Finally it holds position in
front of the surface, long enough for several images to be captured, since a
single frame is rarely enough to decode a label.

The controller also knows when to give up. If the path is cut by something
discovered on the way, or if the vehicle stops making progress towards its
target, the action is released immediately and returns to the common list, where
another vehicle may take it. When a reading succeeds, the label changes state in
the map and the corresponding action disappears from the list.

\Cref{fig:sys:architecture} shows the seven elements and the information that
passes between them. The vehicles observe and write into the map; the map
produces candidate actions; each vehicle prices them, claims one and executes it;
and what the execution establishes returns to the map.

These stages do not run at the same rate. Flight control runs several times per
second, decisions about once per second, and the semantic advice every few
seconds. The loop stops when the inventory stops growing, a condition the team
evaluates for itself.
