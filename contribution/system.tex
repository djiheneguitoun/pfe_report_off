    System for Unknown Warehouses}%

\subimport{.}{intro}


\section{Problem Formulation}%
\label{sec:sys:problem}

\subsection{The Inspection Task}%
\label{subsec:sys:task}

An inventory inspection compares what is physically present on the shelves with
the records held by the \gls{abb:wms}. Keeping these two views consistent is what
makes the operation necessary, since miscounts and misplaced items reduce order
accuracy and raise operating costs~\cite{pore2026drone}. The task takes as input
a bounded space containing stored items, each carrying a visual identifier on its
faces, and returns the list of identifiers actually found, together with the
position at which each was read. Two properties determine whether that list is
usable. It must account for as large a share of the stored items as possible,
since an item that is not counted is an item the records will continue to report
incorrectly. And it must contain no identifier that does not exist in the
warehouse, since a single spurious entry places the whole list in doubt.

\subsection{Operating Conditions}%
\label{subsec:sys:conditions}

The task is carried out under conditions that no design can change. They are
stated here as facts, and the requirements of the following section follow from
them.

\textbf{The space}: No description of the building is available at take-off. The
number of racks, their position and the location of the items are unknown, and
the free volume is bounded by fixed obstacles, racks, columns and pallets left on
the floor, as well as by moving ones, operators and handling
vehicles~\cite{pore2026drone, pore2026uavqr}. A typical warehouse is a large
building whose racks can exceed ten metres in
height~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}, and the spacing between them is tight enough
to force conservative speed planning~\cite{pore2026drone}. The contents of the
space also change while the mission runs: items are deposited and removed during
the operating day, and an aisle that was clear a minute earlier can be
obstructed. Since neither the layout nor its evolution is known beforehand, no
route through the space and no division of that space between vehicles can be
established before the flight.

\textbf{The reading}: A visual identifier is decodable only within a bounded range
of distances, within a bounded viewing angle and below a bounded relative
speed~\cite{dong2024motionblurred, alsulami2026deep}, so a surface can be
observed without its label being read. Lighting and occlusion narrow that range
further: decoding accuracy falls from 98.5\% in a well-lit environment to 85.2\%
in low light and to 78.3\% when the code is partly
hidden~\cite{alsulami2026deep}, and the motion of the platform produces blur that
lowers the recognition rate~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}. The total number of
items present is not known before the mission, and nothing in the environment
signals that the inventory is complete.

\textbf{The fleet}: The endurance of a single vehicle is bounded, and falls below
what the coverage of a working warehouse demands: small \glspl{abb:uav} typically
fly for twenty to thirty minutes~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}, while a complete
manual inventory check consumes more than three days of warehouse
activity~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}. Several vehicles therefore operate in the
same space, which includes aisles wide enough for only one of them at a time.
Using more than one also shortens the mission, since two drones halved the
inspection time of a single one in a warehouse experiment~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. A
vehicle can stop flying at any moment, from a technical failure or a depleted
battery, and without warning~\cite{chandran2024network}. Perception and decision
run on the same limited onboard computer, and the mission depends on both, a
combination identified in the literature as an open
difficulty~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.

\subsection{Requirements and Constraints}%
\label{subsec:sys:requirements}

From these conditions follow seven results that a solution is expected to
produce. They are stated as outcomes that can be verified on a completed mission
rather than as mechanisms, so that they remain independent of any particular
design.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item The inventory is completed without any description of the layout, any
          route, and any division of work being supplied before take-off.
    \item No identifier is reported that does not correspond to an item present
          in the warehouse.
    \item When a vehicle stops flying, the area it was covering is inspected by
          the remaining vehicles before the mission ends.
    \item Two vehicles do not travel to the same objective, so that the capacity
          of the fleet is not spent twice on a single reading.
    \item No vehicle collides with a surface, with an obstacle that appeared
          after take-off, or with another vehicle.
    \item The mission ends at a moment the system determines by itself, without
          an external instruction, and without spending the remaining flight
          budget on a search that no longer produces readings.
    \item The computation performed for each decision, together with the
          perception running alongside it, remains within the onboard budget and
          within the endurance of the platform.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Position of Existing Approaches}%
\label{subsec:sys:position}

No existing system produces these seven results together. Systems designed for
warehouse inspection receive the layout before take-off and compute their
coverage route once, on the ground, leaving the vehicle to execute
it~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, pore2026uavqr, cristiani2020inventory,
    alsulami2026deep, kwon2020robust, guinand2021uavugv}. What matters is not how
that route is computed but when: the decision is made when the system knows the
least it will ever know, and a target appearing later cannot be taken into
account, because the component that produced the plan is no longer
running~\cite{krayani2023goal}. The same design carries two further assumptions.
The environment is treated as immobile, although operators and handling vehicles
move through a working warehouse, and only about 3\% of inventory studies
consider a dynamic one~\cite{pore2026drone}. And cooperation between flying
vehicles is almost absent: where several vehicles are used, the ground robot
carries the drone or places beacons rather than reading any code
itself~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, guinand2021uavugv}.

The methods that could decide during the flight are not designed for this task.
Those that coordinate several vehicles fix the division of labour before
take-off, by a central schedule~\cite{guinand2021uavugv}, a fixed
split~\cite{pore2026uavqr}, or a leader broadcasting
waypoints~\cite{chandran2024network}, and allocation able to adapt in real time
is still described as a future direction rather than a current
practice~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Semantic reasoning appears in two
separate places, and never where it would be needed. On a ground robot working
alone, it does drive exploration, but the mission solves Object Goal Navigation
and ends as soon as one instance of a designated target is
reached~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm, yu2023l3mvn}, and the representation built for
that target cannot be reused for another~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm}. On a drone, it
only interprets an instruction given by a person: the vehicle carries out what it
has been asked to do and never chooses where to explore~\cite{chen2024typefly}.
Semantic reasoning, cooperation and flight therefore never occur together; three
of the four combinations exist, and the missing one is exactly what an inventory
requires.

The two sides therefore never meet: the systems that perform the right task
cannot react, and the systems that can react were not built for it and stop at
the first success. Four of the seven results are ruled out by that alone. An
inventory cannot be completed without a layout when the layout is what the
planner consumes. The area of a vehicle that stops cannot be reassigned when the
assignment was made once. An obstacle appearing after take-off cannot be avoided
by a route fixed before it existed. And a system that ends at the first detection
cannot decide when an inventory is complete. One caveat applies throughout: these
limitations are established almost entirely on simulated missions, since about
45\% of inventory studies are simulation-based against roughly 7\% that report
real experiments~\cite{pore2026drone}.
\section{Overview of the Proposed Approach}%
\label{sec:sys:overview}

The proposed system performs warehouse inventory inspection in an environment whose layout is not known in advance. Three UAVs operate inside the warehouse without a pre-existing map or a pre-planned trajectory. Instead, they progressively build a shared representation of the environment from their observations and use this representation to decide where to move next.

The mission follows a continuous perception--decision--execution loop. The UAVs observe the environment, update the shared map, generate candidate actions from the current state of the map, select actions according to their expected value and cost, and execute them. The results of the execution are then added to the map, which changes the set of available actions for the next decision.

This design removes the need to know the warehouse configuration before take-off. A newly discovered obstacle can modify the traversable space and invalidate a previously selected path. A change in the warehouse can replace information already stored in the map. Similarly, if a UAV stops operating, the action it was performing is eventually released and can be selected by another UAV. The system therefore adapts to changes during the mission rather than relying on a fixed plan.

The approach combines three properties that are central to the proposed system. First, the three UAVs cooperate through a shared representation and redistribute work during the mission. Second, exploration and inventory inspection are performed without prior knowledge of the warehouse layout. Third, a pre-trained vision-language model provides semantic guidance during the decision process. The system is composed of seven main elements, illustrated in \Cref{fig:sys:architecture}.

\subsection{Perception}
\label{sec:sys:perception}

The perception pipeline separates two tasks that cannot be reliably performed at the same distance: \emph{finding} a label and \emph{reading} it.

A \gls{abb:qr} code can only be decoded when the UAV is sufficiently close and observes the code within an appropriate range of viewing angles. A decoder alone would therefore provide no useful information about labels that have not yet been approached. The system first uses a learned detector to identify cartons and \gls{abb:qr} codes from several metres away, without attempting to decode them.

The detected objects are then placed in the environment using the range information provided by the UAV's distance sensor. Once the UAV reaches an appropriate position and orientation, the decoder performs the actual reading.

This creates three states for a label:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.35em]
\item \textbf{Unknown}: the label has not yet been detected.
\item \textbf{Spotted}: the label has been detected and localized, but has not yet been decoded.
\item \textbf{Read}: the label has been successfully decoded.
\end{itemize}

The \emph{spotted} state is particularly important for planning. It provides a concrete target that the system can turn into a candidate reading action.

\subsection{Shared Map}
\label{sec:sys:map}

All three UAVs operate on a single shared map. Each UAV writes new observations to this map and consults the same representation before making its next decision. There is therefore no separate private map that must later be synchronized with the rest of the team.

The map maintains four types of information:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.35em]
\item \textbf{Traversability}: which parts of the volume are known to be free, occupied, or still unknown, allowing paths to be computed through known free space;
\item \textbf{Observed surfaces}: which surfaces have been observed and from which viewing directions, since observing one side of a rack does not guarantee visibility of labels on the opposite side;
\item \textbf{Inventory information}: the detected labels, their positions, and their current states;
\item \textbf{Team state}: the positions of the other UAVs and the actions they have currently claimed.
\end{itemize}

The shared representation has two important consequences. An observation made by one UAV immediately becomes available to the other two, allowing the team to reuse information instead of repeating the same observation. At the same time, new observations can replace previous information when the environment changes. The map therefore represents the current state of the warehouse rather than a fixed model created before the mission.

\subsection{Candidate Actions}
\label{sec:sys:candidates}

The map is not directly used to generate a complete route. Instead, it is used to identify \emph{candidate actions}: locations that are currently worth visiting.

Three types of actions are generated:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
\item \textbf{Reading}: approach a label that has been detected but not yet decoded. The UAV must reach a suitable position and orientation in front of the label.


\item \textbf{Covering}: observe a known surface from a new direction when the current observations are insufficient to determine whether labels are present on that surface. This allows the system to discover labels that have not yet been detected.

\item \textbf{Exploring}: move towards a boundary between the known and unknown regions of the map. Such a boundary is referred to as a \emph{frontier} and provides a classical mechanism for exploring unknown space~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm}.


\end{itemize}

Although these actions have different purposes, they are represented in the same form: a \textbf{position to occupy} together with a \textbf{direction to observe}. This common representation allows reading, covering, and exploration to be evaluated together rather than being handled by separate planning modes.

\subsection{Common Action Value}
\label{sec:sys:scoring}

Once candidate actions have been generated, the system evaluates them using a common numerical value. No fixed priority is imposed between reading, covering, and exploration.

The value of a candidate combines three factors:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.35em]
\item \textbf{Expected return}: the expected contribution of the action to the inventory mission. Reading an already detected label provides a direct inventory entry, while exploration mainly increases the known region and may lead to future discoveries.


\item \textbf{Execution cost}: the cost of reaching the candidate through the currently known free space. The cost is based on the computed path length rather than straight-line distance, so that obstacles and rack geometry are taken into account.

\item \textbf{Coordination penalty}: a penalty applied when another UAV has already claimed the same action or is operating close to it.


\end{enumerate}

A UAV selects the highest-valued candidate among the actions that it can actually reach. Reachability is verified by computing a path through the currently known free space before the action is selected.

This common scale allows the system to balance exploitation and exploration continuously. When unread labels are available, reading actions can naturally obtain higher values. When no such actions remain, exploration can become preferable without explicitly switching the system between predefined operating modes.

\subsection{Claiming and Releasing}
\label{sec:sys:claims}

Coordination is performed through the shared map rather than through a central task allocator.

When a UAV selects an action, it writes the selected action into the shared map as a \emph{claim}. The other UAVs see this claim during their next decision and reduce the value of candidates located close to the claimed action. They are therefore encouraged to select different areas.

A claim is temporary. The UAV must periodically renew it while working on the corresponding action. If the UAV stops renewing the claim, it expires automatically.

This mechanism provides both task coordination and basic fault tolerance:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.35em]
\item it reduces redundant behaviour by discouraging multiple UAVs from selecting the same target;
\item it automatically releases unfinished work when a UAV stops operating.
\end{itemize}

No dedicated failure manager is required. If a UAV stops flying, its claims eventually expire and the corresponding actions return to the common candidate set. The remaining UAVs can then select these actions during their normal decision process. Fault recovery is therefore a consequence of the same claim-expiration mechanism used for ordinary coordination.

\subsection{Semantic Guidance}
\label{sec:sys:semantic}

The geometric decision process is complemented by a pre-trained vision-language model (VLM). At regular intervals, a UAV provides the model with a compact description of the current warehouse state and asks which area should receive attention next and from which side it should be approached.

The VLM does not directly control the UAV or generate its trajectory. Instead, its output modifies the value of candidate actions located in the recommended area. Semantic information therefore influences the same decision mechanism used by geometric information.

This provides information that is difficult to obtain from geometry alone. A geometric map can indicate where the team has not yet explored, but it cannot determine whether one unexplored area appears more promising than another based on its visual content. Semantic guidance can, for example, favour an area containing visible cartons over an apparently empty aisle.

The VLM is also optional by design. Its contribution is controlled by a weighting parameter. Setting this weight to zero removes the semantic contribution and produces a purely geometric system. In addition, VLM requests are performed asynchronously. If a response is not available when a decision is required, the UAV continues using the geometric decision process rather than waiting for the model.

\subsection{Execution}
\label{sec:sys:execution}

After an action has been selected, the UAV controller executes the corresponding position and observation requirement in three stages.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.35em]
\item \textbf{Transit}: the UAV moves rapidly along the computed path through known free space.


\item \textbf{Final approach}: the UAV reduces its speed while approaching the target in order to limit overshoot and reach the required position accurately.

\item \textbf{Observation hold}: the UAV maintains its position and orientation in front of the target long enough to acquire multiple camera frames for reliable QR decoding.


\end{enumerate}

Execution is continuously monitored. If a newly discovered obstacle invalidates the path, or if the UAV fails to make sufficient progress towards the target, the current action is released and becomes available to the other UAVs. When a QR code is successfully read, its state in the shared map changes to \emph{read}, and the corresponding reading action is removed from the candidate set.

\subsection{Overall Mission Loop}
\label{sec:sys:loop}

The seven elements form a single closed loop, as illustrated in \Cref{fig:sys:architecture}.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.35em]
\item The UAVs observe the environment through the perception pipeline.
\item The observations update the shared map.
\item The map produces reading, covering, and exploration candidates.
\item Candidate values are computed using expected return, execution cost, and coordination information.
\item Each UAV selects and claims one reachable action.
\item The controller executes the selected action and monitors its progress.
\item The resulting observations update the shared map, starting the process again.
\end{enumerate}

The different parts of the system operate at different time scales. Flight control runs continuously at a high frequency, action selection is performed approximately once per second, and semantic guidance is requested only every few seconds. This separation allows the VLM to provide higher-level guidance without interfering with the real-time control loop.

The mission terminates when the inventory no longer grows, meaning that the team has reached a state in which additional actions do not produce new inventory entries.
