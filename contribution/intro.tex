\section{Introduction}%
\label{sec:sys:intro}

\Cref{chap:background} established that the platform and the perception pipeline
are no longer the limiting factors in warehouse inventory inspection. Multirotor
vehicles operate reliably in confined indoor spaces, and vision-based
\gls{abb:qr} decoding is accurate enough for the task. The limitation lies
elsewhere, in the assumptions these systems make before take-off: that the layout
of the warehouse is known, that it remains unchanged for the duration of the
mission, and that a single vehicle is sufficient. \Cref{chap:nav} examined the
layer at which these assumptions are made, the decision layer that determines
where a vehicle goes next, and identified four gaps. Work allocation between
several vehicles is always decided before take-off and never revised in flight.
Systems that select their next target from the meaning of the observed scene
operate as a single agent. The few systems that explore an unmapped space
autonomously have been validated only on ground robots. And those systems address
a task that terminates at the first successful detection, whereas an inventory
mission must cover the entire space.

The system designed in this chapter addresses these four gaps within a single
architecture. Several vehicles cooperate inside a warehouse whose layout is
discovered during the mission rather than supplied beforehand. The coverage plan
normally computed on the ground is replaced by a single representation shared by
the whole team: every observation is written into it, and every decision is taken
from it. From that representation the vehicles derive three classes of target,
corresponding to reading a label that has already been spotted, observing a
surface never seen from a readable angle, and extending the known region. All
three are expressed in one common form, a pose and a viewing direction, and are
scored on a single scale, so that reading a label competes directly against
exploring an unobserved area instead of being governed by separate rules. Because
the targets are produced continuously from a shared representation rather than
assigned once before take-off, they move between vehicles while the mission runs:
a vehicle announces the target it has selected, the announcement expires unless
it is renewed, and this mechanism alone allows the team to absorb the loss of a
member without any dedicated failure detection. A pre-trained vision-language
model is integrated into the same loop and advises the team on where to search
next, a role that has so far been evaluated only on single ground platforms. To
the best of our knowledge, no published system combines in-flight target
redistribution, exploration of an unknown warehouse, and semantic guidance within
the same architecture.

The chapter follows the order in which the system is constructed.
\Cref{sec:sys:problem} formulates the problem and derives the requirements a
solution must satisfy. \Cref{sec:sys:overview} presents an overview of the system
and the connections between its components.
\Cref{sec:sys:map,sec:sys:perception,sec:sys:targets,sec:sys:coordination,sec:sys:semantic,sec:sys:control}
develop each component in turn: the shared map, the two-track perception, the
generation and scoring of targets, the coordination mechanism, the semantic
guide, and the flight controller. \Cref{sec:sys:mission} reassembles them and
follows a complete mission from take-off to termination.
\Cref{sec:sys:discarded} reports two approaches explored before this design and
the reasons they were set aside. \Cref{fig:sys:architecture} summarises the
architecture developed in the following sections.
