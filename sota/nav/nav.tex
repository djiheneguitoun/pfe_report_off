\section{Path Planning, Trajectory Design and Decision-Making for UAVs}%
\label{sec:nav:planning}

\subsection{Foundations}%
\label{subsec:nav:foundations}

This subsection establishes the vocabulary, the structuring concepts, and the
taxonomy used throughout the rest of \Cref{sec:nav:planning}.

\paragraph{Path planning, trajectory design, and decision-making.}
These three terms describe related but distinct problems:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Path planning} produces a geometric route from a start position
          to a goal, that is, a sequence of waypoints in space that avoids
          obstacles. The output has no time dimension and does not specify when
          the \gls{abb:uav} passes each point.
    \item \textbf{Trajectory design} turns a geometric route into a
          time-parameterized motion that the \gls{abb:uav} can execute. It assigns
          a velocity profile and respects the dynamics of the vehicle, including
          maximum speed, acceleration, and turn rate.
    \item \textbf{Decision-making} operates step by step. At each instant, the
          \gls{abb:uav} observes its environment, updates an internal estimate of
          the world, and selects the next action. Its output is a policy that maps
          states or beliefs to actions~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.
\end{itemize}
Path planning and trajectory design are \emph{open-loop}: the solution is computed
in advance and executed as is. Decision-making is \emph{closed-loop}: a fresh
action is selected at every step, conditioned on the most recent observation.

\paragraph{Three structuring axes.}
Three distinctions cut across the literature:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Point-to-point versus coverage.} Point-to-point planning targets
          a single goal or a discrete set of goals, whereas coverage planning
          requires that every point of a region be visited.
    \item \textbf{Global versus local.} Global planning operates on the whole map
          and produces a complete route, while local planning runs on board at
          high frequency and reacts to obstacles or events that were not known in
          advance.
    \item \textbf{Offline versus online.} Offline planning is computed once before
          the mission, while online planning is re-computed during the flight as
          new observations arrive. This axis is aligned with the open-loop versus
          closed-loop distinction.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A taxonomy of methods.}
Krayani et~al.~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected} classify \gls{abb:uav} trajectory
design algorithms into three families:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Classical algorithms:} deterministic search and exact
          optimization on a known workspace.
    \item \textbf{Biologically inspired metaheuristics:} population-based
          stochastic search inspired by natural processes.
    \item \textbf{Advanced learning-based methods:} policies learned from
          interaction with the environment.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Classical algorithms}%
\label{subsec:nav:classical}

Classical algorithms originate in robotics and operations research, and they form
the first of the three families identified in \Cref{subsec:nav:foundations}. They
share one defining assumption: the workspace is fully known and either static or
only slowly changing, which lets the planning problem be cast as deterministic
graph search or exact optimization over that known
structure~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. The remainder of this subsection reviews
the representatives most often used in the \gls{abb:uav} literature, from dynamic
programming to combinatorial multi-target tours.

\subsubsection{Dynamic programming (DP)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} \gls{abb:dp} is an optimization method that solves a problem
by breaking it into a sequence of smaller subproblems whose solutions are combined to
build the global one. Its objective is to find the globally optimal sequence of
decisions over multiple stages.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item The problem is divided into stages (typically time steps).
    \item At each stage, all possible states and their costs are enumerated.
    \item The optimal decision at each state is computed by reasoning backwards from
          the final stage.
    \item The optimal global solution is reconstructed by following the chain of
          optimal decisions from the start.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used to plan \gls{abb:uav} trajectories under time constraints, ensuring that
          the drone reaches all targeted hotspots within a fixed mission
          duration~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Applied to \gls{abb:uav}-based data collection from wireless sensor nodes,
          where the algorithm picks the visiting order under time
          constraints~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Returns the globally optimal solution by exact backward induction. & Complexity grows quickly with the number of hovering nodes, limiting real-time use~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Well suited to problems with a clear stage structure. & Does not handle fine-grained grids efficiently~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\

        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Dijkstra's algorithm}

\paragraph*{Definition.} Dijkstra is a graph-search algorithm whose objective is to
find the shortest path between two nodes in a weighted graph with non-negative edge
weights.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item The starting node is assigned a distance of $0$; all other nodes are assigned
          $\infty$.
    \item The unvisited node with the smallest known distance is selected.
    \item The distances of its neighbors are updated (relaxation) if a shorter path is
          found through it.
    \item The process repeats until the destination node is reached.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Computes the shortest route between two locations on a discretized workspace,
          where nodes are candidate positions and edges carry travel costs such as
          distance or energy consumption~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Applied to battery-aware \gls{abb:uav} missions, where the algorithm finds
          the route that reaches the target in the shortest time given a fixed energy
          budget~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Returns the globally optimal shortest path between two nodes. & Requires the graph to be fully known in advance. \\
        Deterministic, well understood, and easy to implement. & Assumes a static environment---any change in edge cost forces a full re-execution from scratch. \\
        Composable as a subroutine inside larger planners. & Runtime grows with the size of the graph, limiting real-time replanning on large discretizations~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        & Solves only single-source shortest path---does not handle multi-target visiting or coverage. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{A* algorithm}

\paragraph*{Definition.} A* is an informed graph-search algorithm that extends Dijkstra
with a heuristic. Its objective is to find the shortest path to a goal while exploring
fewer nodes than Dijkstra by guiding the search toward the goal.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item For each node $v$, define $g(v)$, the known cost from the start to $v$, and
          $h(v)$, a heuristic estimate of the remaining cost to the goal.
    \item The priority of node $v$ is $f(v) = g(v) + h(v)$.
    \item The node with the smallest $f(v)$ is selected for expansion.
    \item The values $g$ and $f$ of its neighbors are updated.
    \item The process repeats until the goal is selected.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used to identify suitable node pairs and compute the shortest path between
          them in a known static environment, ensuring that the path follows the
          shortest direction toward the target~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Applied as a fast point-to-point subroutine inside larger \gls{abb:uav}
          mission planners.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Optimal under an admissible heuristic, while exploring fewer nodes than Dijkstra. & Assumes a static environment---impractical when conditions change during the flight~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Fast in practice on large grids, given a well-designed heuristic. & Performance depends heavily on the quality of the heuristic. \\
        Easy to integrate as a point-to-point subroutine. & Does not produce a continuous trajectory; outputs a sequence of waypoints~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{D* and its variants}

\paragraph*{Definition.} D* and its variants---D*, Focused D*, and D* Lite---form a
family of replanning graph-search algorithms that extend A* to dynamic environments.
Their objective is to update an existing path locally when the cost map changes,
instead of replanning everything from scratch.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item An initial path is computed with A* on the known cost map.
    \item The \gls{abb:uav} starts executing the path.
    \item When a cost change is detected (a new obstacle, an edge becoming
          impassable), only the affected nodes have their cost updated.
    \item The change is propagated locally to repair the existing path.
    \item The \gls{abb:uav} resumes execution on the patched path.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used as an efficient replanning tool in cluttered \gls{abb:uav} environments,
          where new obstacles or cost changes appear during the
          mission~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Allows the \gls{abb:uav} to reuse a previously computed path rather than
          recomputing it entirely after each update~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Handles dynamic cost maps without restarting from scratch. & The quality of the solution is no longer guaranteed in very large or highly dynamic environments~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Much faster than re-running A* after each update. & Still assumes the map is observable and updates are reliable. \\
        Reuses previously computed information. & Remains a point-to-point planner---no coverage, no multi-target. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Mixed-integer linear programming (MILP)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} \gls{abb:milp} is an optimization framework that mixes
continuous variables (positions, velocities) and integer variables (typically binary,
for discrete decisions). Its objective is to find the globally optimal trajectory under
linear constraints by solving a branch-and-bound problem.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item The \gls{abb:uav} trajectory is discretized into time steps.
    \item Continuous variables encode the \gls{abb:uav} state at each step (position,
          velocity).
    \item Binary variables encode discrete choices (which side of an obstacle, which
          waypoint to activate).
    \item The objective function and the constraints (dynamics, obstacles, time) are
          written linearly.
    \item A branch-and-bound solver returns the globally optimal trajectory.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used to plan \gls{abb:uav} trajectories in discrete time steps, where each
          step represents the dynamic state of the \gls{abb:uav} in the
          environment~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Designed for offline planning, producing a feasible trajectory before the
          \gls{abb:uav} starts executing its task~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Returns the globally optimal trajectory under linear constraints. & Tends to get stuck because of its blind, exhaustive nature~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Naturally encodes integer choices (waypoint, obstacle side). & Cannot generate long trajectories in complex environments without severe scaling issues~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Explicit, interpretable optimization formulation. & Offline by design---no online adaptation during the mission. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Traveling salesman problem (TSP) and its profit variant (TSPWP)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} The \gls{abb:tsp} is a combinatorial routing problem whose
objective is to find the shortest tour that visits each location exactly once and
returns to the starting point. The profit-augmented variant, the \gls{abb:tspwp}, adds
a reward at each location, and the objective balances minimizing travel cost and
maximizing collected reward.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item The set of locations to visit and the pairwise travel costs $c_{ij}$ are
          defined.
    \item For the \gls{abb:tspwp} only, a reward $r_j$ is associated with each
          location.
    \item An initial tour is built (e.g., random permutation or nearest-neighbor
          heuristic).
    \item A local-search heuristic (2-opt, 3-opt) iteratively improves the tour by
          swapping small portions of the route.
    \item The process stops when no further improvement is found.
\end{enumerate}
The \gls{abb:tspwp} objective combines the two terms:
\begin{equation}
    \min \quad \alpha \sum_{(v_i, v_j) \in E} c_{ij}\, x_{ij}
    \;-\; \beta \sum_{v_j \in V} r_j\, y_j ,
    \label{eq:nav:tspwp}
\end{equation}
where $x_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether edge $(v_i, v_j)$ is in the tour,
$y_j \in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether location $j$ is visited, and $\alpha + \beta = 1$
trades off cost against reward~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used to plan \gls{abb:uav} trajectories when the mission consists of visiting
          a fixed set of locations within a battery-limited time
          window~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item The \gls{abb:tspwp} has been applied to \gls{abb:uav}-assisted wireless
          networks, where the \gls{abb:uav} chooses which hotspots to serve, balancing
          travel cost against the data-rate reward of each
          hotspot~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Direct formulation of multi-target missions (visit each location once, return to base). & NP-hard---exact solutions intractable for realistic instance sizes~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        The \gls{abb:tspwp} captures non-uniform priorities through the reward term. & Offline by design---a new target forces a full replan from the start~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Near-optimal solutions reachable in reasonable time with 2-opt\,/\,3-opt. & Does not handle vehicle dynamics or partial observability. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Biologically inspired metaheuristics}%
\label{subsec:nav:metaheuristics}

When the planning problem is non-convex, NP-hard, or simply too large for the exact
methods of \Cref{subsec:nav:classical}, the literature turns to biologically
inspired metaheuristics---the second family identified in
\Cref{subsec:nav:foundations}. These algorithms replace exact optimization with
population-based stochastic search inspired by natural processes, trading the
guarantee of global optimality for the ability to explore very large and irregular
search spaces. The three most common in \gls{abb:uav} path planning are the genetic
algorithm, particle swarm optimization, and ant colony
optimization~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.

% Each acronym is introduced in its subsubsection title below; unset the first-use
% flag so the body and tables render the short forms (GA, PSO, ACO).
\glsunset{abb:ga}\glsunset{abb:pso}\glsunset{abb:aco}

\subsubsection{Genetic algorithm (GA)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} \gls{abb:ga} is a population-based optimization method
inspired by natural selection. Its objective is to find a near-optimal solution by
evolving a population of candidate solutions through selection, crossover, and mutation
across multiple generations.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item An initial population of candidate solutions (chromosomes) is generated,
          often randomly.
    \item Each candidate is evaluated using a fitness function derived from the cost.
    \item Parents are selected with probability proportional to their fitness.
    \item Crossover combines pairs of parents to produce offspring that mix their
          characteristics.
    \item Mutation randomly perturbs some offspring to maintain diversity.
    \item The new offspring replace the worst members of the population.
    \item The process repeats over multiple generations until convergence.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used to obtain near-optimal \gls{abb:uav} paths in target-coverage problems
          and combinatorial routing tasks such as \gls{abb:tsp}-style trajectory
          design~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Frequently combined with other metaheuristics (e.g., \gls{abb:pso}) to
          balance global search and local refinement~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Explores large, non-convex solution spaces well. & Convergence is relatively slow, which limits time-critical \gls{abb:uav} missions~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Works on non-differentiable, non-linear cost functions. & No guarantee of optimality. \\
        Easy to adapt to different mission types by changing the fitness function. & Whenever the mission changes, the search must restart from scratch. \\
        Naturally parallelizable across the population. & Tuning operators (crossover rate, mutation rate, population size) is non-trivial. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Particle swarm optimization (PSO)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} \gls{abb:pso} is a population-based optimization method
inspired by the social behavior of bird flocks and fish schools. Its objective is to
find a near-optimal solution by having a swarm of particles move through the solution
space under the joint influence of their own best and the swarm's best.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item A swarm of particles is initialized with random positions and velocities in
          the solution space.
    \item Each particle's position represents a candidate solution and is evaluated
          using the cost function.
    \item Each particle remembers its personal best position $p_i$.
    \item The swarm shares the global best position $g$ found so far.
    \item At each iteration, every particle updates its velocity using:
\end{enumerate}
\begin{equation}
    v_i^{t+1} = \omega\, v_i^{t} + c_1 r_1 \bigl(p_i - x_i^{t}\bigr)
    + c_2 r_2 \bigl(g - x_i^{t}\bigr),
    \label{eq:nav:pso}
\end{equation}
then updates its position as $x_i^{t+1} = x_i^{t} + v_i^{t+1}$, where $\omega$ is an
inertia weight, $c_1$ and $c_2$ are cognitive and social coefficients, and
$r_1, r_2 \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$. The process repeats until convergence to the global
best.

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used for local optimization of \gls{abb:uav} trajectories, where fast
          convergence around a known region is needed~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Frequently combined with \gls{abb:ga}, with \gls{abb:ga} driving the global
          search and \gls{abb:pso} refining locally~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Variants such as spherical-vector \gls{abb:pso} have been applied to
          safety-enhanced \gls{abb:uav} path planning.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Faster convergence than \gls{abb:ga} for local refinement~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. & Can get trapped in local optima, especially in highly multimodal landscapes. \\
        Few parameters to tune compared to \gls{abb:ga}. & No guarantee of optimality. \\
        Naturally parallelizable across the swarm. & Like all metaheuristics, must be re-run when the mission changes. \\
        Easy to combine with other metaheuristics (hybrid \gls{abb:ga}--\gls{abb:pso}). & Performance depends on careful tuning of inertia and acceleration coefficients. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Ant colony optimization (ACO)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} \gls{abb:aco} is a population-based optimization method
inspired by how real ants find the shortest path between a nest and a food source
through pheromone trails. Its objective is to find near-optimal solutions to
combinatorial routing problems by indirect communication between artificial ants via a
shared pheromone matrix.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item A colony of artificial ants is placed at the starting node.
    \item Each ant builds a candidate solution (a path) by choosing the next node
          probabilistically, biased toward edges with stronger pheromone and shorter
          distance.
    \item Once all ants have built their solutions, the pheromone on each edge is
          updated: it evaporates partially, and ants reinforce the edges they used in
          their tour.
    \item Better solutions deposit more pheromone, which makes them more attractive in
          the next iteration.
    \item The process repeats until convergence to a high-quality tour.
\end{enumerate}
The pheromone update on edge $(i,j)$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{ij}^{t+1} = (1 - \rho)\, \tau_{ij}^{t}
    + \sum_{k=1}^{m} \Delta\tau_{ij}^{k},
    \label{eq:nav:aco}
\end{equation}
where $\rho \in (0,1)$ is the evaporation rate, $m$ is the number of ants, and
$\Delta\tau_{ij}^{k}$ is the pheromone deposited by ant $k$ on edge $(i,j)$.

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used to solve \gls{abb:uav} path-planning problems formulated as
          combinatorial routing tasks (\gls{abb:tsp}-like)~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Applied to coverage path planning on grid-discretized areas, including a
          chaotic \gls{abb:aco} variant for cooperative coverage with multiple
          \glspl{abb:uav}.
    \item Naturally adapted to multi-agent settings, since the pheromone trail provides
          indirect communication between drones.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Finds good solutions for combinatorial routing problems. & Requires a large number of iterations to converge---slow~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Naturally suited to multi-agent (multi-\gls{abb:uav}) coordination through shared pheromones. & High simulation time and computational cost~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Adaptive---the pheromone trail can reinforce successful behaviors over time. & Performance sensitive to the evaporation rate and the pheromone\,/\,heuristic balance. \\
        Robust to local changes through pheromone dynamics. & Like other metaheuristics, no optimality guarantee. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Advanced learning-based methods}%
\label{subsec:nav:learning}

Advanced learning-based methods form the third and last family identified in
\Cref{subsec:nav:foundations}. They replace explicit optimization with a policy
learned from interactions with the environment. The dominant paradigm is
\gls{abb:rl}, which casts the problem as a \gls{abb:mdp} or its partially observable
variant, the \gls{abb:pomdp}, and solves it through value-based, policy-based, or
actor-critic algorithms. This subsection first introduces the \gls{abb:mdp} and
\gls{abb:pomdp} formal foundations, then reviews the main \gls{abb:rl} families used
in \gls{abb:uav} path planning and decision-making.

% Each acronym is introduced in its subsubsection title below; unset the first-use
% flag so the body and tables render the short forms (DQN, MARL, HRL).
\glsunset{abb:dqn}\glsunset{abb:marl}\glsunset{abb:hrl}

\subsubsection{Markov decision process (MDP)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} The \gls{abb:mdp} is the mathematical framework for sequential
decision-making under uncertainty. Its objective is to find a policy that maximizes
the expected cumulative reward over time, assuming full observability of the state.

\paragraph*{Formal definition.} An \gls{abb:mdp} is a tuple
$\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$, where:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space;
    \item $\mathcal{A}$ is the action space;
    \item $P(s' \mid s, a)$ is the state transition probability;
    \item $R(s, a)$ is the reward function;
    \item $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ is the discount factor.
\end{itemize}
A policy $\pi(a \mid s)$ maps states to action distributions. The objective is to
maximize the expected discounted return:
\begin{equation}
    J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t\, R(s_t, a_t)\right].
    \label{eq:nav:mdp-return}
\end{equation}

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item At each time step, the agent observes the current state $s_t$.
    \item It selects an action $a_t$ according to its policy $\pi$.
    \item The environment transitions to a new state $s_{t+1}$ following
          $P(s' \mid s, a)$.
    \item The agent receives a reward $r_t = R(s_t, a_t)$.
    \item The policy is updated to maximize the long-term return.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Partially observable MDP (POMDP)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} The \gls{abb:pomdp} extends the \gls{abb:mdp} to environments
where the agent cannot directly observe the state. Its objective is to find a policy
that maximizes the expected cumulative reward based on a belief over states, rather
than the state itself.

\paragraph*{Formal definition.} A \gls{abb:pomdp} is a tuple
$\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \Omega, O, \gamma)$, where the
additional elements are:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item $\Omega$ is the observation space;
    \item $O(o \mid s', a)$ is the observation probability.
\end{itemize}
The agent maintains a belief state $b_t$, which is a probability distribution over
$\mathcal{S}$, updated through Bayesian inference at each step.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item At each time step, the agent receives a partial observation $o_t$, not the
          true state.
    \item It updates its belief $b_t$ over possible states using Bayes' rule.
    \item It selects an action $a_t$ based on the belief $b_t$.
    \item The environment transitions; a new observation $o_{t+1}$ is received.
    \item The belief is updated again, and the cycle continues.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used in \gls{abb:uav} navigation for active perception of targets in
          uncertain and cluttered environments, where sensor noise and occlusion make
          direct state observation impossible~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.
    \item Solved online with samplers such as \gls{abb:pomcp} or the
          \gls{abb:abt}~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Q-learning}

\paragraph*{Definition.} Q-learning is a model-free, off-policy \gls{abb:rl} algorithm
whose objective is to learn the action-value function $Q^\pi(s, a)$, i.e., the
expected return of taking action $a$ in state $s$ and following $\pi$ thereafter.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item The Q-values $Q(s, a)$ are initialized arbitrarily for all state-action
          pairs.
    \item At each step, the agent observes state $s_t$, selects an action $a_t$ (e.g.,
          $\varepsilon$-greedy on $Q$), and receives reward $r_t$ and next state
          $s_{t+1}$.
    \item The Q-value is updated using the Bellman equation:
\end{enumerate}
\begin{equation}
    Q(s_t, a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t, a_t) + \alpha \Bigl[\, r_t
    + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a') - Q(s_t, a_t) \,\Bigr].
    \label{eq:nav:qlearning}
\end{equation}
The process repeats until convergence, and the optimal policy is recovered as
$\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s, a)$.

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used for \gls{abb:uav} trajectory design to maximize sum-rate, \gls{abb:qoe},
          or user-coverage fairness in wireless
          networks~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Multi-agent Q-learning has been applied to coordinate several \glspl{abb:uav}
          in user-mobility prediction tasks~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Model-free---no need to know the environment dynamics. & Suffers from the curse of dimensionality: table size grows exponentially with state variables~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        Converges to the optimal Q-function under mild conditions. & Cannot handle continuous state or action spaces directly. \\
        Easy to implement and well understood. & Tied to a specific environment---generalizes poorly to unseen scenarios~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Deep Q-network (DQN) and its variants}

\paragraph*{Definition.} The \gls{abb:dqn} extends Q-learning by replacing the Q-table
with a neural network $Q(s, a; \theta)$. Its objective is to scale Q-learning to
high-dimensional state spaces (e.g., raw sensor data).

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item The Q-function is parameterized by a neural network with weights $\theta$.
    \item The agent interacts with the environment and stores transitions
          $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in a replay buffer.
    \item Minibatches are sampled from the buffer to break temporal correlations.
    \item A target network with frozen weights $\theta^-$ is used to compute stable
          targets.
    \item The network is trained to minimize the loss:
\end{enumerate}
\begin{equation}
    L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta^-)
    - Q(s, a; \theta)\bigr)^2\right].
    \label{eq:nav:dqn}
\end{equation}
The target network is periodically updated to track $\theta$.

\paragraph*{Variants.} Double DQN reduces overestimation bias by decoupling action
selection from value estimation; Dueling DQN separates state value and action
advantage in the network architecture.

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used to provide \gls{abb:uav} network service to ground users in rapidly
          changing environments~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Applied to spectrum sharing and dynamic resource allocation for
          \gls{abb:uav} swarms~\cite{ding2024external}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Handles high-dimensional discrete action spaces. & Training requires a large number of samples. \\
        Replay buffer and target network stabilize training. & Restricted to discrete actions only. \\
        Once trained, inference is fast. & Online learning is sensitive to the strong correlation between successive samples~\cite{ding2024external}. \\
        & Generalizes poorly to environments not seen during training~\cite{arshid2026active}. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Policy-gradient methods: PPO, DDPG, and actor-critic}

\paragraph*{Definition.} Policy-gradient methods form a family of \gls{abb:rl}
algorithms that directly optimize a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ instead
of learning a value function. Their objective is to handle continuous action spaces
(\gls{abb:uav} velocity, heading) and obtain smoother policies.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item A policy $\pi_\theta$ is parameterized by a neural network (the actor).
    \item A value function $V_\phi$ or $Q_\phi$ is parameterized by another network
          (the critic).
    \item The agent collects trajectories by executing $\pi_\theta$.
    \item The critic estimates the advantage $\hat{A}_t = R_t - V_\phi(s_t)$.
    \item The actor is updated to increase the probability of actions with positive
          advantage.
    \item The critic is updated to better predict the return.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{Main variants used in the UAV literature.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \gls{abb:ppo} uses a clipped objective to prevent destructive policy updates:
          \begin{equation}
              L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\min\bigl(r_t(\theta)\,
              \hat{A}_t, \; \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)\,
              \hat{A}_t\bigr)\right],
              \label{eq:nav:ppo}
          \end{equation}
          where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)$.
    \item \gls{abb:ddpg} learns a deterministic policy $\mu_\theta(s)$ in an
          actor-critic architecture with target networks and experience replay; suited
          to continuous control such as \gls{abb:uav} trajectory
          optimization~\cite{ding2024external}.
    \item \gls{abb:td3} improves \gls{abb:ddpg} stability through twin critics and
          delayed policy updates.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \gls{abb:ddpg} used for \gls{abb:uav} trajectory optimization in dynamic
          spectrum sharing networks, with offline experience replay improving data
          efficiency~\cite{ding2024external}.
    \item \gls{abb:ppo} applied to \gls{abb:uav} trajectory optimization for spectrum
          cartography~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}.
    \item Actor-critic combined with attention mechanisms for resource allocation in
          multi-\gls{abb:uav} vehicular networks.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Naturally handles continuous action spaces. & Very high training cost, often hundreds of thousands of episodes~\cite{ding2024external}. \\
        Smoother and more stable than \gls{abb:dqn} in continuous control. & Online learning is data-inefficient and tends to fall into suboptimal solutions when the environment varies~\cite{ding2024external}. \\
        \gls{abb:ppo} is robust to hyperparameter choices. & Sensitive to reward shaping; poor reward design leads to poor policies. \\
        \gls{abb:ddpg} and \gls{abb:td3} are well suited to \gls{abb:uav} trajectory optimization~\cite{ding2024external}. & Generalization to unseen environments is limited; retraining often required~\cite{arshid2026active}. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} \gls{abb:marl} extends \gls{abb:rl} to systems with multiple
interacting agents. Its objective is to learn coordinated policies for several
\glspl{abb:uav} operating in the same environment.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Each \gls{abb:uav} is modeled as an agent with its own observation and action
          space.
    \item Agents can be centralized (a single controller with full information) or
          decentralized (each agent acts on its local view).
    \item A common paradigm is \gls{abb:ctde}: training uses global information, but at
          execution each agent acts on its local observations only.
    \item Algorithms include \gls{abb:ma2c}, \gls{abb:maddpg}, and hybrid frameworks
          such as MA2C-DDPG for mixed discrete-continuous
          decisions~\cite{ding2024external}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used to optimize spectrum allocation and \gls{abb:uav} trajectory jointly in
          anti-jamming networks, with \gls{abb:ma2c} handling discrete spectrum
          decisions and \gls{abb:ddpg} handling continuous trajectory
          updates~\cite{ding2024external}.
    \item Applied to dynamic resource allocation in multi-\gls{abb:uav} vehicular
          networks.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Captures interactions and cooperation between multiple \glspl{abb:uav}. & Non-stationary environment from each agent's viewpoint (other agents change too). \\
        \gls{abb:ctde} provides a practical balance between coordination and on-board execution~\cite{ding2024external}. & Training scales poorly with the number of agents. \\
        Naturally fits multi-\gls{abb:uav} mission scenarios. & Strong coupling between agents complicates credit assignment. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL)}

\paragraph*{Definition.} \gls{abb:hrl} is an \gls{abb:rl} framework that decomposes a
complex task into a hierarchy of sub-tasks, each governed by its own policy. Its
objective is to handle long-horizon \gls{abb:uav} missions by separating high-level
decisions (which sub-task to execute) from low-level ones (how to execute it).

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item The task is decomposed into options (or sub-policies), each representing a
          temporally extended action.
    \item A high-level policy selects which option to execute given the current state.
    \item The chosen option is executed by its own low-level policy until a termination
          condition is met.
    \item Control returns to the high-level policy for the next option selection.
\end{enumerate}
The Option-Critic architecture uses four learning updates: option-selection policy,
option-value function, termination policy, and option-intra-action policy.

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used for multi-\gls{abb:uav} collaborative \gls{abb:dnn} model inference,
          where the high-level policy decides where to partition the model and the
          low-level policy executes the corresponding layer~\cite{arshid2026active}.
    \item Applied in long-horizon \gls{abb:uav} missions where the sub-task structure
          is well defined.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Handles long-horizon tasks by decomposing them into reusable sub-policies. & Requires defining the option structure, which is task-specific. \\
        Improves sample efficiency by reusing sub-policies. & Training is more complex than flat \gls{abb:rl}. \\
        Naturally fits hierarchical mission structures (mission $>$ sub-task $>$ primitive action). & Termination conditions and option discovery remain open research issues. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{POMDP online solvers: POMCP and ABT}

\paragraph*{Definition.} \gls{abb:pomcp} and the \gls{abb:abt} are sampling-based
algorithms that solve \glspl{abb:pomdp} in real time by combining Monte Carlo tree
search with belief tracking. Their objective is to compute an approximately optimal
policy on the fly without solving the full \gls{abb:pomdp} offline.

\paragraph*{How does it work?}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item The agent maintains a set of particles representing its belief over states.
    \item At each decision step, it runs Monte Carlo simulations from the current
          belief, exploring the most promising action sequences.
    \item The simulations use the \gls{abb:pomdp} model as a generative simulator (no
          need to enumerate the full state space).
    \item After a budget of simulations, the agent selects the action with the highest
          estimated value.
    \item After execution, the belief is updated based on the new observation, and the
          search resumes from the updated belief.
\end{enumerate}
\gls{abb:pomcp} restarts the search at each step. The \gls{abb:abt} reuses the
previously computed policy and only updates the affected branches when changes in the
model are detected~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.

\paragraph*{UAV applications.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item Used for autonomous \gls{abb:uav} navigation in cluttered,
          \gls{abb:gps}-denied environments to find targets under sensor
          uncertainty~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.
    \item The \gls{abb:abt} is preferred over \gls{abb:pomcp} for real-world conditions
          where the environment changes gradually, since it avoids discarding previous
          computation at each step~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Strengths and limitations.}
\begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Strengths} & \textbf{Limitations} \\
        \midrule
        Solves \glspl{abb:pomdp} online, without offline policy computation. & Performance depends on the number of simulations per step. \\
        Handles continuous state spaces through particle approximations. & Quality of the belief depends on the number and diversity of particles. \\
        The \gls{abb:abt} reuses prior computation, reducing waste between steps~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}. & Requires a generative model of the environment, which may be hard to build. \\
        Well-suited to cluttered, partially observable \gls{abb:uav} scenarios~\cite{sandino2020autonomous}. & Latency may still be too high for high-frequency control loops. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}


\section{Active Inference for UAV Systems}%
\label{sec:nav:activeinf}

\Gls{abb:aif} is a unified probabilistic framework that combines perception,
prediction, and action selection under a single objective: minimizing free
energy. Originally proposed in computational neuroscience by Friston, it has
progressively been adopted in robotics and, more recently, in \gls{abb:uav}
systems. This section reviews the foundations of \gls{abb:aif} and the way it has
been instantiated for \gls{abb:uav} state estimation, control, and
decision-making, in single-agent and multi-agent settings. It then identifies the
gaps that remain in the current literature.

\subsection{Theoretical foundations}%
\label{subsec:nav:aif-foundations}

The free-energy machinery rests on a single principle and two derived
quantities---one for perceiving the present, one for planning the future---which
can in turn be instantiated in a continuous or a discrete form. These four
building blocks are introduced below.

% FEP is first named in the subsubsection title just below, so unset its
% first-use flag to render the short form in the body. VFE and EFE are first used
% in the body of this subsection and are left to expand on first use.
\glsunset{abb:fep}

\subsubsection{The Free Energy Principle (FEP)}

The \gls{abb:fep} is a theoretical framework rooted in neuroscience and
statistical physics. It states that any self-organizing system, biological or
artificial, survives by keeping its sensory inputs close to what it expects to
perceive. To this end, the agent carries an internal generative model of its
environment and tries to make the world match the model in two complementary
ways:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Perception:} updating its beliefs when the observations
          contradict the model.
    \item \textbf{Action:} acting on the world to bring the observations back in
          line with the model.
\end{itemize}
The two mechanisms run together in a continuous closed
loop~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected, pan2026active}.

In principle, the quantity the agent should minimize is \emph{surprise}, that is,
how unexpected its current sensory input is. Surprise cannot be computed directly,
however, because it requires integrating over every possible cause of the
observation. \Gls{abb:aif} turns the principle into a usable algorithm by
replacing surprise with a tractable upper bound called \emph{free energy}, and by
splitting it into two complementary forms: \gls{abb:vfe} for inference at the
current time step, and \gls{abb:efe} for planning into the
future~\cite{pan2026active, ji2024integration}.

\subsubsection{Variational Free Energy (VFE) for perception}

\Gls{abb:vfe} is the quantity the agent minimizes to figure out where it is and
what is going on around it. It measures how far the agent's current beliefs about
the hidden state of the world are from the true posterior given the observations.
Each time a new observation arrives, the agent adjusts its beliefs so that
\gls{abb:vfe} decreases, which in practice means moving its internal estimate
closer to the most plausible explanation of what it has just sensed.

Formally, letting $z_t$ denote the agent's belief about the hidden state, $x_t$
the true hidden state, and $y_t$ the observation, \gls{abb:vfe} is defined as the
Kullback--Leibler divergence between the approximate posterior $q(z_t)$ and the
true posterior $p(x_t \mid y_t)$~\cite{ji2024integration}:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{AIF}} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(z_t)\,\|\,p(x_t \mid y_t)\bigr)
    - \ln p(y_t, a_t).
    \label{eq:nav:vfe}
\end{equation}
Minimizing $F_{\text{AIF}}$ with respect to the belief $z_t$ yields state
inference, while minimizing it with respect to the action $a_t$ yields active
control~\cite{ji2024integration}. \Gls{abb:vfe} is also an upper bound on the
model evidence: pulling free energy down is equivalent to making the agent's model
explain its observations better.

\subsubsection{Expected Free Energy (EFE) for action selection}

While \gls{abb:vfe} handles inference at the current step, \gls{abb:efe} handles
planning into the future. For each policy---each candidate sequence of
actions---the agent computes how much free energy that policy would generate if
executed, and selects the policy with the lowest expected value. \Gls{abb:efe}
breaks naturally into two terms with intuitive
meaning~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected, pan2026active}:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Pragmatic term:} how well the policy is expected to achieve the
          agent's preferred outcomes (its goals).
    \item \textbf{Epistemic term:} how much the policy is expected to reduce
          uncertainty about the world.
\end{itemize}
This decomposition is one of the central appeals of \gls{abb:aif}: a single
objective unifies goal-directed behaviour (exploitation of preferences) and
information-seeking behaviour (exploration to reduce uncertainty), without
requiring an external reward signal as in
\gls{abb:rl}~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected, pan2026active}.

\subsubsection{Continuous and discrete Active Inference}

The same \gls{abb:vfe} and \gls{abb:efe} machinery can be instantiated in two
different ways, depending on whether the states and actions are continuous or
discrete:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.6em]
    \item \textbf{Continuous \gls{abb:aif}} is used when hidden states and actions
          live in a continuous space (positions, velocities, accelerations,
          control inputs) and when the dynamics and observations can be
          approximated as Gaussian. In that regime, \gls{abb:vfe} minimization
          collapses into a Kalman-filter-like update for perception, and
          \gls{abb:efe} minimization collapses into a Proportional--Integral
          controller for action~\cite{ji2024integration, ji2023delay}. This is the
          natural choice for low-level control, where the question is \emph{how} to
          follow a trajectory.
    \item \textbf{Discrete \gls{abb:aif}} is used when states and actions take
          discrete values (visiting hotspot~A or hotspot~B, selecting the motion
          primitive \emph{up} or \emph{down}, switching from nominal to evasive
          mode). The generative model becomes a \gls{abb:pomdp}, policies are
          sequences of discrete actions, and free energy is computed over
          them~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected, arshid2026active}. This is the
          natural choice for high-level decision-making, where the question is
          \emph{which} trajectory to follow.
\end{itemize}
Both formulations are used in \gls{abb:uav} systems. In practice, several recent
works combine them in a hierarchical architecture: discrete \gls{abb:aif} on top
to plan the mission, and continuous \gls{abb:aif} at the bottom to execute the
motion.

\subsection{Discrete AIF for trajectory planning and decision-making}%
\label{subsec:nav:aif-discrete}

Once the question shifts from \emph{how} to follow the trajectory to \emph{which}
trajectory to follow, the decisions become discrete choices among finite options:
which target to visit next, which region to traverse, which motion primitive to
apply, which spectrum band to use. The setting is then modeled as a
\gls{abb:pomdp}, where the \gls{abb:uav} maintains beliefs over discrete hidden
states that represent the mission, the current route, or the active threat. What
turns this \gls{abb:pomdp} into a tractable \gls{abb:aif} problem is a structure
that recurs across the entire \gls{abb:uav} corpus: a world model learned offline
from expert demonstrations, encoded as a hierarchical \gls{abb:gdbn}. At
deployment, the \gls{abb:uav} does not search the action space from scratch; it
scores candidate decisions by their \gls{abb:efe} on the learned world model,
which gives it a way to generalize to scenarios that were not present in the
training data without retraining. \Cref{fig:nav:aif-discrete-recipe} summarizes
this offline-to-online recipe.

\subsubsection{Goal-directed trajectory in wireless networks}

The founding instance of this recipe asks a \gls{abb:uav} to visit a set of
hotspots within a battery time window. Offline, a \gls{abb:tspwp} solver refined
by 2-OPT is run on many randomized hotspot configurations, and the resulting
trajectories serve as expert demonstrations from which the \gls{abb:uav} learns a
coupled multi-scale world model. The model captures two scales jointly: hotspot
order at the event scale, and inter-hotspot motion at the slot scale. Online, the
\gls{abb:uav} infers the most plausible plan for the current configuration and
scores actions by their \gls{abb:efe}, achieving faster convergence and stronger
generalization than Q-learning~\cite{krayani2023goaldirected}. A refinement keeps
the same world model and replaces direct \gls{abb:efe} scoring with an
expected-surprise criterion, measured as the Bhattacharyya distance between
predicted and reference observations, which further speeds up adaptation to new
scenarios without retraining~\cite{krayani2024selfsupervised}.

\subsubsection{Adapting to adversarial conditions}

The recipe extends naturally when the environment becomes hostile. Against ground
jammers whose positions are not known in advance, the world model is enriched with
a third dictionary on top of region sequences and motion primitives: it encodes
the typical \gls{abb:sinr} feedback the \gls{abb:uav} should expect at each region
under nominal and jammed conditions. Online, the agent minimizes \gls{abb:efe}
jointly across the three levels, switching motion primitives between nominal and
evasive behaviour and reacting to \gls{abb:sinr} feedback to detect and localize
the jammers. A Kalman-filter-based attractor at the motion layer smooths the
trajectory between symbolic switches. The framework reaches the performance of an
expert that knows the jammer positions in advance, while Q-learning collapses as
jammer density grows~\cite{krayani2026bayesian}. The same logic has been applied
earlier at the spectrum layer rather than the motion layer, with an Active
\gls{abb:gdbn} capturing joint signal--jammer dynamics and the action being the
choice of frequency band that minimizes an abnormality measure equivalent to free
energy~\cite{krayani2022novel}.

\subsubsection{Scaling to a cooperating swarm}

The recipe also scales beyond a single \gls{abb:uav}. For a swarm that must
cooperatively visit a set of targets under inter-agent distance constraints,
expert demonstrations are produced offline by a \gls{abb:ga} with Repulsion
Forces, and the world model is structured into three abstraction levels: a Mission
level that assigns subsets of targets to individual \glspl{abb:uav}, a Route level
that determines each \gls{abb:uav}'s visiting order, and a Motion level that
produces dynamically feasible inter-target segments. Each \gls{abb:uav} runs its
own \gls{abb:aif} inference on the shared model structure, and an Extended Kalman
Filter at the motion layer prevents collisions when the world model and the real
environment drift apart. Compared to a Q-learning baseline trained on the same
expert data, the framework converges faster and yields lower mission times with
smoother and safer trajectories under static and dynamic
obstacles~\cite{arshid2026active}.

\subsection{AIF in multi-UAV and multi-agent settings}%
\label{subsec:nav:aif-multiagent}

When \gls{abb:aif} is extended from a single \gls{abb:uav} to a coordinated team,
the technical question is no longer how each agent minimizes its own free energy,
but what each agent must share with the others for the group as a whole to remain
coherent. The free-energy objective itself does not specify a coordination
mechanism, so the literature has explored three distinct things that
\glspl{abb:uav} can exchange: their current intentions, their world model, or the
wireless resources through which they observe each other.

\subsubsection{Sharing intentions}

The lightest form of coordination is to have each \gls{abb:uav} broadcast the
policy it is about to execute and let its peers fold this intention into their own
preferences. Coordination then emerges without any explicit consensus protocol:
every \gls{abb:uav} continues to minimize its own free energy, but its preference
distribution now includes the intentions of others, which gradually steers the
group toward compatible behaviors. This logic has been embedded into \gls{abb:bt}
execution semantics, where deterministic branching is replaced by
free-energy-driven policy selection that takes peer intentions as additional
priors. The resulting framework keeps the modular and interpretable structure of
conventional \glspl{abb:bt} while cutting node complexity by more than 70\% under
partially observable cooperative tasks~\cite{wang2026bridging}.

\subsubsection{Sharing the world model}

A stronger form of coordination has all \glspl{abb:uav} reason on the same
hierarchical world model. The shared structure typically encodes coordinated
behavior at three abstraction levels (mission allocation, route ordering, motion
segments), and each \gls{abb:uav} independently minimizes the KL divergence
between its current belief and the reference distribution at every level. Because
they all reason on the same generative model, their individual decisions remain
compatible by construction, without any centralized controller. An Extended Kalman
Filter at the motion layer absorbs the inevitable drift between the world model and
the real environment, and the approach produces smoother and safer trajectories
than \gls{abb:rl} baselines trained on the same data~\cite{arshid2026active}.

\subsubsection{Sharing sensing resources}

A third direction shifts the question from coordinating \glspl{abb:uav} to
coordinating the infrastructure that observes them. The \gls{abb:uav} dynamics,
the wireless sensing base station, and the resource allocation across users are
encoded together in a single factor-graph generative model, so that \gls{abb:vfe}
gives the state estimate and \gls{abb:efe} jointly selects the next control input
and the next sensing allocation. The experimental setup is single-\gls{abb:uav},
but the formulation extends naturally to fleets in which the base station
distributes its sensing budget across multiple agents according to a shared
free-energy objective, and it consistently outperforms greedy and random
allocation strategies~\cite{pan2026active}.

\section{Generative AI Integration for UAV Systems}%
\label{sec:nav:genai}

The rapid progress of \glspl{abb:llm}, \glspl{abb:vlm}, and deep generative
models has opened a new layer of possibilities for autonomous \gls{abb:uav}
systems. Most of this literature does not focus on \glspl{abb:uav} specifically,
but on how these models can be integrated into robotic agents at different levels:
as planners that translate language into action, as perception modules that enrich
what the agent sees, as policies that grow over time, or as deep formulations of
free energy that scale Bayesian reasoning to neural networks. The following
subsections extract the general patterns that emerge from this literature, detail
how they work, and discuss in the final subsection which ones can be transposed to
a \gls{abb:uav}-based warehouse inspection system.

\subsection{Language as a high-level control interface}%
\label{subsec:nav:genai-language}

The first pattern uses an \gls{abb:llm} as the top layer of an otherwise classical
control stack. The operator types a command in natural language, the \gls{abb:llm}
converts it into a compact plan made of robot primitives, and a runtime executes
that plan step by step while watching its progress. Keeping the latency acceptable
on a real drone (under 1.5~seconds between the command and the first action in
TypeFly) imposes three design choices: a target language denser than Python to
save tokens, a streaming interpretation that executes each instruction as soon as
it arrives instead of waiting for the full plan, and replanning triggers that call
the \gls{abb:llm} again only when the situation actually drifts from the plan.

\paragraph*{Pipeline.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Input:} user command in English plus the drone video stream.
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} a lightweight visual encoder (typically YOLOv8) runs on
          an edge server and turns the video into a textual scene description (a
          list of objects with bounding boxes).
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} a prompt generator combines the task, the scene
          description, the target-language syntax (for instance MiniSpec), and a
          few few-shot examples.
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} the cloud \gls{abb:llm} streams back a plan, instruction
          by instruction.
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} a runtime parses each instruction as it arrives and
          translates it into drone API calls.
    \item \textbf{Step 5:} the runtime can call the \gls{abb:llm} back through a
          \emph{probe} function when the scene is under-specified, or through a
          \emph{replan} function when an action fails.
    \item \textbf{Output:} the actual drone trajectory that realizes the task.
\end{itemize}

This architecture is represented by TypeFly with its MiniSpec language and its
streaming runtime~\cite{chen2024typefly}, and by ChatGPT for Robotics, which
follows the same logic using Python as the target language and a high-level
function library specific to the robotic platform~\cite{vemprala2023chatgpt}. The
general pattern is that the \gls{abb:llm} does not replace low-level control or
metric planning: it sits on top and translates human intent into structured
commands, while the rest of the stack remains that of a classical drone.

\subsection{Grounding language in what the agent can actually do}%
\label{subsec:nav:genai-grounding}

\Gls{abb:llm}-generated actions are often linguistically plausible but physically
infeasible: opening a closed door, flying through a wall, using an object that is
not within reach. The grounding pattern addresses this through a two-stage
decision loop in which the \gls{abb:llm} proposes several candidate actions in
natural language and a second component scores them by feasibility in the current
state, keeping only the most realistic among those that are actually executable.

\paragraph*{Pipeline.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Input:} the current robot observation plus a task in natural
          language.
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} the \gls{abb:llm} generates $k$ candidate actions in
          natural language (typically $k = 10$), each with a linguistic
          probability.
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} for each candidate, a pre-trained value function (a
          Q-network trained by \gls{abb:rl} on elementary robotic skills) estimates
          the probability of success in the current state.
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} the decision combines the two scores, usually by
          product: $\text{score} = p_{\text{LLM}}(\text{action}) \times
          p_{\text{value}}(\text{action} \mid \text{state})$.
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} the action with the highest score is executed by the
          low-level controller.
    \item \textbf{Step 5:} the loop restarts with the new observation.
    \item \textbf{Output:} a sequence of executable and linguistically relevant
          actions.
\end{itemize}

The reference work is SayCan~\cite{ahn2022saycan}, which uses an \gls{abb:llm}
(PaLM) for the linguistic component and value functions trained by \gls{abb:rl} on
the skills of a mobile manipulator for the feasibility component. The general
pattern is that the output of an \gls{abb:llm} should never be consumed raw by an
embodied robot: it must always pass through a filter that reflects the agent's
actual capabilities in the current state, otherwise the agent will attempt to
execute invalid actions.

\subsection{Vision-language models as semantic sensors}%
\label{subsec:nav:genai-perception}

A second pattern uses \glspl{abb:vlm} as a perception layer that produces
structured semantic statements about the scene, rather than raw geometry
(occupancy, depth). The literature uses this layer in two complementary forms,
depending on whether the categories of interest are known in advance or not.

\paragraph*{Pipeline --- known categories (lightweight detector).}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Input:} an RGB image from the onboard camera.
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} a compact detector (YOLOv8 fine-tuned on the target
          categories) outputs a list of bounding boxes annotated with a class and a
          confidence score.
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} a post-processor turns this list into a structure
          consumable by the belief module (for example, a dictionary
          \texttt{\{"shelf\_3\_row\_2": "QR detected, confidence 0.94"\}}).
    \item \textbf{Output:} structured semantic observations ready to be fused with
          the current belief.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Pipeline --- open categories (full VLM).}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Input:} an RGB image plus a textual question
          (\enquote{describe what you see on this shelf}).
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} a \gls{abb:vlm} (GPT-4V, Claude Vision, LLaVA,
          MiniCPM-V) receives the image and the question.
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} the \gls{abb:vlm} produces a natural-language answer,
          more or less free depending on the prompt.
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} a parser extracts from the answer the relevant entities,
          attributes, and anomalies (missing objects, misplaced items, unexpected
          ones).
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} the result is converted into the same structured format
          as for the lightweight detector.
    \item \textbf{Output:} high-level semantic observations, potentially novel,
          that go beyond what a pre-trained detector could recognize.
\end{itemize}

This pattern appears in partial form in TypeFly through YOLOv8~\cite{chen2024typefly}
and in a more open form in ChatGPT for Robotics, which dialogues with the user
about the perceived scene~\cite{vemprala2023chatgpt}. The general pattern is that
robotic perception should no longer stop at the geometric level: generative models
offer a symbolic layer that can be consumed directly by the decision module.

\subsection{Skill libraries that grow with experience}%
\label{subsec:nav:genai-skills}

A third pattern, demonstrated in simulated environments and not yet applied to
\glspl{abb:uav}, is the idea of an agent whose action repertoire grows over time.
Instead of being limited to a fixed set of primitives, the agent stores each
successful behavior as a reusable skill, indexed for later retrieval in similar
situations.

\paragraph*{Pipeline.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Input:} an open environment where the agent can test actions,
          plus an \gls{abb:llm} that generates code.
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} an automatic curriculum module asks the \gls{abb:llm} to
          produce the next task adapted to the agent's current state
          (\enquote{find a wooden object}, \enquote{build a stone pickaxe}).
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} the \gls{abb:llm} generates code (typically JavaScript
          or Python) that calls existing skills and environment primitives to solve
          the task.
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} this code is executed; runtime errors and environment
          feedback (observable state, event log) are fed back into a prompt that
          asks the \gls{abb:llm} to correct it.
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} the loop continues until a self-verification module
          confirms the task is solved.
    \item \textbf{Step 5:} the final code is embedded (for example via
          \texttt{text-embedding-ada-002}) and stored in a vector store indexed by
          a semantic description of the skill.
    \item \textbf{Step 6:} on a future task, the retrieval module queries the
          vector store by similarity and suggests relevant skills as building
          blocks.
    \item \textbf{Output:} a skill library that grows mission after mission.
\end{itemize}

This architecture is implemented by Voyager in the Minecraft
environment~\cite{wang2024voyager}. The general pattern is that long-term autonomy
benefits from an explicit memory of already-acquired skills, not only from updated
model parameters: a learned skill remains accessible and reusable as is.

\subsection{Scaling free-energy reasoning to deep models}%
\label{subsec:nav:genai-deepaif}

The pattern most directly tied to \gls{abb:aif} is the replacement of the
analytical generative model by neural encoders. The agent then maintains four
components parameterized by deep networks (a belief encoder, a prior encoder, a
dynamics model, and a preference model) and selects actions through the
\gls{abb:feef}, which decomposes the planning objective into an exploration term
that rewards uncertainty reduction and an exploitation term that rewards reaching
preferred outcomes.

\paragraph*{Pipeline.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Input:} the current environment observation.
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} the belief encoder $h_\theta$ projects the observation
          $o_t$ into a latent belief $s_t = h_\theta(o_t)$.
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} for each candidate action $a_t$, the dynamics model
          $d_\theta$ predicts the future belief $s_{t+1} = d_\theta(s_t, a_t)$.
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} the prior encoder $h'_\theta$ predicts the expected
          belief independently of the action, providing a reference against which
          to measure surprise.
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} the preference model $p_\theta$ evaluates how well the
          future belief matches the agent's preferred outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Step 5:} the \gls{abb:feef} combines the two terms:
          \begin{equation}
              \text{FEEF}(a) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(s_{t+1} \,\|\, s'_{t+1}\bigr)
              - \mathbb{E}\bigl[\log \tilde{p}(\tilde{o}_t)\bigr],
              \label{eq:nav:feef}
          \end{equation}
          where the first term is the divergence between the prediction and the
          prior (exploration) and the second is the log-likelihood of preferred
          observations (exploitation).
    \item \textbf{Step 6:} the action with the lowest \gls{abb:feef} is selected
          and executed.
    \item \textbf{Step 7:} the transition is added to a replay buffer prioritized
          by \gls{abb:feef} values, which stabilizes learning by concentrating
          updates on the most informative transitions.
    \item \textbf{Output:} a learned policy that explicitly balances exploration
          and exploitation.
\end{itemize}

This scheme is instantiated by Fang et~al.\ with an application to video-diffusion
task offloading~\cite{fang2024resource}. The general pattern is that \gls{abb:aif}
does not need to remain analytical: it can scale to neural architectures while
keeping its principled exploration\,/\,exploitation split, and it then outperforms
classical deep \gls{abb:rl} baselines (\gls{abb:ppo}, \gls{abb:sac},
\gls{abb:ddpg}, \gls{abb:td3}) on convergence and asymptotic performance.

\subsection{Patterns transposable to a warehouse inspection system}%
\label{subsec:nav:genai-transposable}

Of the five patterns presented, three transpose directly to a \gls{abb:uav}-based
warehouse inspection system. \emph{Language as a high-level control interface}
would let an operator formulate missions without scripting (\enquote{inspect
aisle~3 and skip empty shelves}), with the \gls{abb:llm} producing a decomposition
into sub-tasks that the underlying decision module executes. \emph{Grounding} is a
necessary condition for this first brick: the sub-tasks generated by the
\gls{abb:llm} must be projected onto the finite set of actions actually executable
by the drone before being executed. \emph{Vision-language perception}, in turn,
would turn the onboard camera into a semantic sensor producing structured
observations (\enquote{missing pallet}, \enquote{unreadable \gls{abb:qr}},
\enquote{unexpected object}) that complement the \gls{abb:lidar}-based occupancy
belief.

The other two patterns are more indirect. The deep formulation of free energy with
multi-step rollouts traces a path toward evolving a single-step \gls{abb:aif}
planner into a learnable decision module aligned with the same objective, but it
assumes a volume of training data and a GPU infrastructure that exceed the scope of
a first prototype. The skill library belongs to a longer-term strategy, relevant
for missions repeated in similar warehouse configurations, but it offers no
immediate gain as long as the basic skills are not yet stabilized.
