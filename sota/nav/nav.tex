\section{Open-Loop and Closed-Loop Navigation}%
\label{sec:nav:principles}

\subsection{Principle of Open-Loop Navigation}%
\label{subsec:nav:openloop-principle}

In open-loop navigation, the complete path is resolved before the drone starts
moving. The planner takes as input a map of the environment, a start position,
and a target~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}, then searches for the route that
minimises a chosen cost. That cost need not be distance: flight time, energy
consumption, the number of turns, or path smoothness are all used in
practice~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Once the route has been obtained, the
vehicle executes it as computed.

This mode of operation is also known as offline planning, and it presupposes a
static environment~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Where that assumption holds, it
offers a real advantage: the problem is solved once, on the ground, without the
computational constraints of the onboard hardware.

\subsection{Principle of Closed-Loop Navigation}%
\label{subsec:nav:closedloop-principle}

In closed-loop navigation, the decision is re-derived throughout the flight. The
drone observes its surroundings, updates its internal representation of them, and
selects its next action from that updated state. Online planning rests on the
same principle and is designed to accommodate unexpected obstacles and changing
conditions~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Learning-based methods make this
structure explicit: the agent executes an action, the environment returns an
observation and a reward, and both are fed back into the following decision,
closing the loop~\cite{chang2023review}.

The benefit is robustness. Fixed waypoints and pre-computed paths adapt poorly to
conditions that evolve and to obstacles that were not anticipated, which raises
the risk of collision~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.



\section{Open-Loop Methods: Classical Algorithms}%
\label{sec:nav:classical}

Classical algorithms come from robotics and operations research, and they form
the first family in the standard taxonomy of trajectory design
methods~\cite{krayani2023goal}. They all share one assumption: the workspace is
known before the flight and does not change during the mission. The first four
methods search for a path inside a graph. The last two describe the mission as an
optimisation problem and solve it with a solver.

\subsection{Dynamic Programming}%
\label{subsec:nav:dp}

Dynamic programming is not really a competitor of the methods that follow. It is
the general principle behind several of them. The idea is to cut a large decision
problem into a series of smaller steps, solve each step one after the other, and
then build the global solution from these partial results.

For \glspl{abb:uav}, it has been used to build a trajectory that fits inside a
fixed mission time, and to choose in which order a drone should visit wireless
sensor nodes to collect data~\cite{krayani2023goal}. Its cost grows quickly with
the number of nodes, which makes it too slow for real time, and it works poorly
on fine grids, because more accuracy means more
computation~\cite{krayani2023goal}.

\subsection{Dijkstra's Algorithm}%
\label{subsec:nav:dijkstra}

Dijkstra looks for the shortest path between two points in a graph. It starts
from the departure point and explores the graph in all directions, like a circle
that grows, until this circle reaches the goal~\cite{chang2023review}. Because it
explores everything around the start, it always finds the shortest path.

It has been used to bring a drone to its target in the shortest time for a given
battery level~\cite{krayani2023goal}. Its advantages are that the result is
guaranteed, that it is simple to implement, and that it works in any
environment~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Its drawbacks come from the same
behaviour: it is slow, it becomes expensive on large graphs, and it only works in
static environments~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.

\subsection{A* Algorithm}%
\label{subsec:nav:astar}

A* improves Dijkstra with one simple idea. Instead of exploring in all
directions, it also estimates how far each point still is from the goal, and it
explores first the points that seem closer to it~\cite{chang2023review}. The
search becomes directed instead of blind, so A* reaches the target after visiting
far fewer points, and it returns a path that is close to the best
one~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.

This gain has a price. A* uses a lot of memory in large environments, and the
quality of the result depends on the quality of the estimate it
uses~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. The path is also not smooth, because the
drone can only move towards a fixed set of neighbouring directions, which gives a
route with sharp turns~\cite{chang2023review}. Finally, A* returns a list of
waypoints, not a trajectory that the vehicle can directly
follow~\cite{krayani2023goal}.

\subsection{D* and Its Variants}%
\label{subsec:nav:dstar}

D* was created to remove the main weakness of the two previous methods. When the
map changes, it does not start again from zero. It only updates the part of the
path that is affected, and it keeps the rest~\cite{krayani2023goal}. This makes
it much faster when new obstacles appear in a cluttered environment.

It also works on maps that are not grids and keeps a low computation
time~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. On the other hand, it needs a good initial
path, it can become expensive in some situations~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive},
and it gives no guarantee on the quality of the result in a large environment
that keeps changing~\cite{krayani2023goal}. As explained in
\Cref{subsec:nav:closedloop-principle}, D* sits on the border of the open-loop
family: it starts from a complete path, like the other methods here, but it
corrects that path during the flight.

The next two methods work differently. They do not search inside a graph. They
write the mission as an optimisation problem and let a solver find the answer.

\subsection{Mixed-Integer Linear Programming}%
\label{subsec:nav:milp}

Linear programming looks for the minimum or the maximum of a linear function
under a set of linear constraints. This fits path planning well, because the goal
is often to minimise a distance, a time, or an
energy~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Mixed-integer linear programming, or
\glsxtrshort{abb:milp}, extends this to problems that mix continuous values, such
as a position, with discrete choices, such as visiting a point or
not~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.

For a \gls{abb:uav}, the flight is cut into time steps, and each step describes
the state of the drone. The solver then returns a complete trajectory before the
mission starts~\cite{krayani2023goal}. This method is designed for offline
planning, and it has two known limits: the search has no guide, so it can get
stuck, and it does not work well for long trajectories in complex
environments~\cite{krayani2023goal}.

\subsection{Traveling Salesman Problem and Its Profit Variant}%
\label{subsec:nav:tsp}

The travelling salesman problem, or \glsxtrshort{abb:tsp}, is used when the
mission is not to reach one goal but to visit several places. The drone must pass
through a fixed set of points, visit each one only once, and come back to its
starting position before the battery runs out~\cite{krayani2023goal}.

The problem is NP-hard, which means that finding the exact best tour takes too
much time as soon as the number of points grows. In practice, two solutions are
used: local-search methods such as 2-OPT and 3-OPT, which improve a tour step by
step, or evolutionary algorithms~\cite{krayani2023goal}. The \gls{abb:tspwp} is a
version where each point also carries a reward, and the algorithm then looks for
the best compromise between the distance travelled and the reward
collected~\cite{krayani2023goal}. This is useful when some targets matter more
than others. Its main limit is important for our case: the \glsxtrshort{abb:tsp}
is offline, so if a new target appears during the flight, the whole tour must be
recomputed from the beginning~\cite{krayani2023goal}.

Behind their differences, these methods share the same limitation. All of them
need a description of the workspace before the flight, as a graph, a grid, or a
list of targets with known costs. This is exactly the assumption that does not
hold in our mission, where the layout is discovered while the drone inspects it.
Even D*, the most flexible of the six, still starts from a complete path and only
repairs it. The next section presents a second group of open-loop methods. They
keep the same assumption, but they replace exact search with stochastic
optimisation.

\section{Open-Loop Methods: Biologically Inspired Algorithms}%
\label{sec:nav:metaheuristics}

The previous section ended on a difficulty: the \glsxtrshort{abb:tsp} is NP-hard,
so exact solutions become impossible to compute as soon as the number of targets
grows. One of the two answers proposed in the literature is to use evolutionary
optimisation algorithms, which have proved effective at reducing the total
distance travelled in real cases~\cite{krayani2023goal}. This is the second
family of open-loop methods.

These algorithms take their inspiration from the collective behaviour of natural
systems, where a group of simple agents interacts to solve a problem
together~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. They do not compute the best solution,
they search for a good one. A population of candidate solutions is generated,
evaluated, and improved over many iterations, until the result stops getting
better. They are useful when the optimal solution is hard to define in advance,
but they give no guarantee of optimality~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm}%
\label{subsec:nav:ga}

The genetic algorithm follows the principle of natural selection. A population of
candidate paths is created, each one is scored, and the best ones are used to
produce the next generation through selection, crossover, and
mutation~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Over the generations, the population
moves towards better routes.

For \glspl{abb:uav}, the genetic algorithm has been used to obtain a suitable
path, in particular in target-coverage problems~\cite{krayani2023goal}. Its main
weakness is speed: convergence is slow, which makes it inefficient for path
planning problems that need a fast answer~\cite{krayani2023goal}, and this
becomes a real obstacle in real-time scenarios~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.

\subsection{Particle Swarm Optimization}%
\label{subsec:nav:pso}

Particle swarm optimisation is inspired by the movement of bird flocks. Each
particle represents a candidate solution and moves through the search space under
two influences: the best position it has found itself, and the best position
found by the whole swarm. Step by step, the swarm concentrates around the
promising regions.

It is widely used in \gls{abb:uav} path planning~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}
and is good at local optimisation, which is why it is often combined with a
genetic algorithm, the latter handling the global search~\cite{krayani2023goal}.
Two limits are reported. Standard versions converge too early and explore a
limited part of the space~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}, which means the swarm
can settle on a solution that is not the best one. Variants have been proposed to
correct this, for example by keeping the population diverse, or by encoding the
motion of the vehicle directly in the particles in order to follow dynamic
targets~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.

\subsection{Ant Colony Optimization}%
\label{subsec:nav:aco}

Ant colony optimisation copies the way ants find the shortest route between their
nest and a food source~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Artificial ants build
candidate paths, and each one leaves a pheromone trail on the route it used.
Short routes receive more pheromone, so they attract more ants in the next
iteration, while the pheromone on the other routes slowly evaporates. The colony
converges on a good tour without any central decision.

The method is effective for \gls{abb:uav} path planning, and it has been applied
to low-altitude planning, to multi-\gls{abb:uav} missions, and to coverage
planning~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Its cost is the main drawback: it needs a
large amount of data to find the optimal solution, its iterations are slow, and it
requires much more simulation time than the other two~\cite{krayani2023goal}. It
also struggles with local optima in complex
scenarios~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.

None of the three dominates the other two, and the literature suggests that
combining them may be necessary to solve \gls{abb:uav} path planning
effectively~\cite{krayani2023goal}. This is why hybrid schemes are common, the
most frequent one pairing a genetic algorithm for the global search with particle
swarm optimisation for local refinement~\cite{krayani2023goal}.

These methods remove the scalability barrier of the exact algorithms, but they do
not remove the assumption behind them. They are optimisers, not decision loops:
the candidate solutions, the cost function, and the set of targets must all be
defined before the search starts. Variants designed for dynamic targets exist,
yet they still re-optimise a model that is given in advance. The next section
gathers the limits shared by the two open-loop families and explains why they are
not sufficient for the mission considered in this work.

\section{Limits of Open-Loop Methods}%
\label{sec:nav:openloop-limits}

The two families presented above differ in the way they search for a solution.
Classical algorithms explore a graph or solve an exact optimisation problem,
while biologically inspired algorithms replace this exact search with a
population that improves over iterations. They do not differ, however, in what
they assume before the search starts. Four limits follow from that shared
assumption.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{The environment must be known in advance.} Path planning begins
          with a model of the environment, in which the obstacles are already
          identified, and with a start point and a target that are already
          fixed~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Whatever form this model takes, a
          graph, a grid, or a list of targets with their costs, it has to exist
          before the flight.
    \item \textbf{The plan does not adapt during the mission.} Offline planning
          assumes a static environment~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}, so a route
          computed on the ground stays valid only as long as nothing changes.
          Fixed waypoints and pre-computed paths are poorly suited to conditions
          that evolve and to obstacles that were not known in advance, and this
          increases the risk of collision~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.
    \item \textbf{Replanning is expensive.} When something does change, most of
          these methods have to start again. With the \glsxtrshort{abb:tsp}, the
          appearance of a single new target forces the whole tour to be
          recomputed from the beginning~\cite{krayani2023goal}. D* is the
          exception, since it repairs only the affected part of the
          path~\cite{krayani2023goal}, but even D* needs a complete initial path
          to repair.
    \item \textbf{Coordination between drones is decided before take-off.} In a
          multi-\gls{abb:uav} mission, the targets have to be assigned and the
          workload balanced~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. With an open-loop
          scheme, this assignment is part of the plan computed on the ground.
          Allocation systems able to learn and adapt to environmental changes in
          real time are presented in the literature as a future direction, not as
          current practice~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.
\end{itemize}

These four limits matter directly for the mission studied in this work. The
warehouse layout is not known before the flight, since the drone discovers it
while inspecting. Obstacles are not all static, because operators and forklifts
move during the mission. New targets appear as the inspection progresses, in the
form of unread codes and unexplored areas. And the work must be shared between
several drones from what they observe, not from a plan fixed in advance. None of
these four conditions is compatible with a route computed once before take-off.

decision as new observations arrive. \Cref{sec:nav:learning} presents the
learning-based approaches, \Cref{sec:nav:activeinf} active inference, and
\Cref{sec:nav:genai} the recent use of language and vision-language models.
