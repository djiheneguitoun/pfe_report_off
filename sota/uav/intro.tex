\section{Chapter Introduction}\label{sec:uav:intro}

Warehouse management is a central link in modern logistics chains. It covers
the operations required to receive, store, track, and ship goods across the
supply chain. Within these operations, inventory management plays a strategic
role: it ensures that the right product is available in the right quantity at
the right time, prevents stockouts and overstock, and directly affects both
customer satisfaction and operational cost~\cite{pore2026drone}.

For decades, inventory has relied on manual methods such as human operators
with handheld scanners, paper-based records, and periodic stocktaking cycles.
These methods are slow, labor-intensive, error-prone, and expose workers to
safety risks, especially when high racks must be inspected. The rise of
Industry~4.0 has accelerated the adoption of more advanced technologies in
warehouses, including \gls{abb:rfid} tags, ground-based mobile robots such as
\glspl{abb:agv} and \glspl{abb:amr}, connected warehouse management systems,
\gls{abb:iot} sensors, and AI-based solutions. While these tools improve
real-time stock visibility, they still face limitations in infrastructure
cost, spatial coverage, and flexibility in dynamic
environments~\cite{pore2026drone}.

In this context, \glspl{abb:uav}, commonly called drones, have emerged as a
promising alternative. Their ability to move vertically, reach high shelves,
and quickly scan large areas makes them well suited to inventory inspection. A
recent review of more than 120 studies shows that drones are now used for a
wide range of warehouse tasks, including inventory counting, mapping,
inspection, surveillance, picking support, and intra-logistics
transport~\cite{pore2026drone}. To identify items, most systems rely on optical
markers placed on products or racks. Among the available
technologies---1D~barcodes, \gls{abb:rfid}, fiducial markers such as ArUco or
AprilTag, and \gls{abb:qr} codes---the \gls{abb:qr} code has progressively
become the standard choice: it stores more information than a classical
barcode, remains readable when partially damaged thanks to its built-in error
correction, is cheap to print, and is well suited to onboard camera reading
from a \gls{abb:uav}.

Operating drones inside warehouses involves several technical challenges.
Warehouses are \gls{abb:gps}-denied environments~\cite{chang2023review}, with
narrow aisles and frequent proximity to human operators, which makes onboard localization a non-trivial problem. Image-based perception is also constrained by the
flying platform itself, since motion blur, oblique viewing angles, variable
altitude, and uneven warehouse lighting all reduce image quality and affect
\gls{abb:qr} code reading~\cite{dong2024algorithm}. When coordination is
extended from a single drone to a swarm, additional aspects come into play,
including task allocation, inter-agent communication, and behavior under
partial failures.

This chapter provides a structured state of the art on \gls{abb:uav}-based
warehouse inventory inspection and the associated \gls{abb:qr} code detection.
It is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:uav:problem} introduces the problem of
warehouse inventory inspection, reviews the limits of traditional methods that
motivate the use of \glspl{abb:uav}, and describes the functional capabilities
of a \gls{abb:uav}-based inspection system. \Cref{sec:uav:detection} focuses
on item detection and identification from drones, with a particular emphasis
on \gls{abb:qr} code detection methods (classical, deep learning, hybrid, and
\gls{abb:uav}-specific pipelines). \Cref{sec:uav:swarm} discusses the move
from single-\gls{abb:uav} to multi-\gls{abb:uav} and swarm architectures, and
reviews the main coordination strategies. \Cref{sec:uav:evaluation} examines the
simulators and co-simulation frameworks used in the community to validate UAV-based
inspection systems, with particular attention to robot--network co-simulation for multi-UAV settings. Finally,
\Cref{sec:uav:conclusion} summarizes the key findings of the chapter and
identifies the main gaps in the literature.
