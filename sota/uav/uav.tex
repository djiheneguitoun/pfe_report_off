\section{Unmanned Aerial Vehicles}%
\label{sec:bg:uav}

\subsection{Definition of a UAV}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-definition}

An \glsxtrfull{abb:uav}, commonly called a drone, is an aircraft that operates
without a pilot on board. It can be controlled remotely by a human operator or
autonomously by software running on the vehicle. A \gls{abb:uav} is also equipped
with sensors selected according to its mission~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}.

\Glspl{abb:uav} are used in many fields, including surveillance, monitoring,
search and rescue, and mapping~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Multirotor
\glspl{abb:uav} are particularly useful for repetitive or risky activities, such
as warehouse inventory management and infrastructure inspection, because they
reduce the need for human involvement~\cite{tejeroruiz2024realistic}.

\subsection{Main Categories of UAVs}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-categories}

The literature generally distinguishes four main types of \glspl{abb:uav}:
fixed-wing, single-rotor, multirotor, and hybrid
platforms~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.

\textbf{Fixed-wing UAVs} use wings to generate lift and are designed for
efficient and fast flight. They offer long endurance and are therefore suitable
for large-area surveys and structure inspection~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.
However, they cannot hover and require a conventional take-off and landing
procedure~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}. These constraints make them unsuitable for
confined indoor environments.

\textbf{Single-rotor UAVs}, similar to helicopters, can hover and carry
relatively large payloads~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}. However, their mechanical
structure is more complex, and they are generally more expensive and difficult to
operate~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.

\textbf{Multirotor UAVs} are widely used for imaging and inspection because of
their low cost, compact size, manoeuvrability, and ability to take off, land, and
hover vertically~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}. Quadrotors are the most common
configuration. Their main limitations are shorter flight time and lower payload
capacity compared with larger platforms~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.

\textbf{Hybrid UAVs} combine characteristics of fixed-wing and rotary-wing
\glspl{abb:uav}. They can perform vertical take-off and landing and can hover,
while also providing better flight efficiency than conventional
multirotors~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}. However, their design and control are more
complex~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.

\subsubsection*{Suitability for Indoor Applications}

Among these types, multirotor \glspl{abb:uav} are particularly suitable for
indoor inspection. Their ability to hover and move freely in three dimensions
allows them to approach objects closely~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}, and their
compact size lets them operate in narrow aisles and at different rack
levels~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Vertical take-off and landing platforms have
therefore become widely used in warehousing
operations~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}, and recent inspection systems rely on
quadrotors in both simulation and real experiments~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. However,
autonomous control remains challenging because the drone must plan and execute
its movements safely in a confined and complex
environment~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.

\subsection{Components of a UAV Platform}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-components}

A \gls{abb:uav} platform combines several subsystems that work together to
provide flight, sensing, computation, and communication.
\Cref{fig:bg:uav-architecture} gives an overview of these subsystems and of the
information exchanged between them.


\subsubsection*{Airframe and Propulsion System}

The airframe and the propulsion chain form the physical layer of the platform.
The airframe carries every other subsystem and fixes the geometry of the vehicle,
while the propulsion chain converts stored electrical energy into the thrust that
lifts it and moves it along the aisles.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Frame}: Provides the mechanical structure of the \gls{abb:uav}
          and supports its main components~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.
    \item \textbf{Motors}: Convert electrical energy into mechanical rotation to
          drive the propellers~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.
    \item \textbf{Propellers}: Generate the thrust required for flight and
          movement. Their number and arrangement define the multirotor
          configuration, with quadrotors being the most
          common~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Flight Control System}

The flight control system is what keeps the vehicle in the air. It closes the
loop between what the sensors measure and what the motors do, and it operates at
three levels: stabilising the attitude, following a requested trajectory, and
planning the mission from which those trajectories are derived.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Flight controller}: Processes sensor measurements and generates
          the commands required to maintain the \gls{abb:uav}'s stability and
          control its movement~\cite{pore2026drone, mohsan2023unmanned}.
    \item \textbf{Autopilot}: Provides higher-level functions such as mission
          planning and waypoint generation~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}. PX4 and
          ArduPilot are widely used open-source autopilot
          stacks~\cite{acharya2020cornet}.
    \item \textbf{Low-level control}: Regulates the \gls{abb:uav}'s altitude,
          velocity, and orientation so that it follows the requested trajectory.
          \Gls{abb:pid} controllers are widely used because they are simple to
          implement~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Onboard Sensors}

Sensing serves two distinct purposes on an inspection platform. One group of
sensors estimates the state of the vehicle itself, its attitude, altitude,
heading and position, while a second group observes the environment in order to
detect obstacles and to read the codes attached to the racks.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{IMU}: Combines an accelerometer and a gyroscope to measure
          linear acceleration and angular motion~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus}.
    \item \textbf{Barometer and magnetometer}: Provide altitude and heading
          information, respectively~\cite{chandran2024network, jacinto2024pegasus}.
    \item \textbf{RGB camera}: Provides visual information and is commonly used
          for barcode and \gls{abb:qr} code detection in warehouse
          applications~\cite{pore2026drone}.
    \item \textbf{Depth camera and LiDAR}: Provide distance information for
          mapping and obstacle detection~\cite{pore2026drone, chang2023review}.
    \item \textbf{ToF sensors}: Measure distance from the travel time of a
          signal, with accuracy decreasing as the distance
          grows~\cite{tejeroruiz2024realistic}. They are therefore suited to
          short-range proximity detection~\cite{pore2026drone}.
    \item \textbf{GNSS/GPS}: Provides global positioning for outdoor
          flight~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus}. Since \gls{abb:gps} is unreliable
          indoors, other positioning methods are required in warehouse
          environments~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Onboard Computing and Communication}

Beyond flight control, an inspection mission needs computing power for perception
and decision, and links to carry the results out of the vehicle. These functions
are kept apart from the flight control unit, so that a heavy processing task
cannot interfere with the stability of the platform.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Companion computer}: Runs the high-level logic of the mission,
          separately from the flight control unit~\cite{acharya2020cornet}.
    \item \textbf{Communication interfaces}: Allow the \gls{abb:uav} to exchange
          information with a ground station and, in multi-\gls{abb:uav} systems,
          with other drones~\cite{chandran2024network, acharya2020cornet}.
    \item \textbf{Wi-Fi and telemetry radio}: Wi-Fi links are commonly used for
          data exchange in warehouse prototypes~\cite{cristiani2020inventory},
          while the flight control unit uses a radio modem for telemetry and
          flight control~\cite{acharya2020cornet}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Power Supply}

All the subsystems above draw on a single onboard energy source, and that source
sets the horizon of the mission. Battery capacity determines how long the vehicle
can fly, and therefore how much of a warehouse one flight can cover before the
drone has to return and recharge.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Battery}: Supplies energy to all \gls{abb:uav} subsystems and
          directly affects flight
          endurance~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive, mohsan2023unmanned}.
    \item \textbf{Flight endurance}: Depends mainly on vehicle weight, size, and
          payload~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}. Small \glspl{abb:uav} typically fly
          for twenty to thirty minutes, so limited battery capacity restricts the
          duration of an inspection mission~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.
    \item \textbf{Extended operation}: Multiple \glspl{abb:uav} can distribute
          the workload~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}, while some systems use
          ground charging stations that the drone visits when its battery falls
          below a threshold~\cite{cristiani2020inventory}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{UAVs in Indoor Environments}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-indoor}

Indoor \gls{abb:uav} operation differs from outdoor flight because warehouses are
confined, \gls{abb:gps}-denied, and often contain both static and moving
obstacles.

Several characteristics of the indoor setting shape the way a platform must
operate. Warehouses are structured and confined spaces, built around narrow
aisles and multi-level racks that leave limited room for
manoeuvring~\cite{pore2026drone, pore2026uavqr}. Indoor conditions also make
\gls{abb:gnss} unreliable, so \glspl{abb:uav} must rely on alternative
localisation methods~\cite{pore2026drone, chang2023review}. Racks, shelves,
workers and forklifts can obstruct the path of the vehicle, which makes real-time
obstacle detection and avoidance necessary~\cite{pore2026drone, pore2026uavqr}.
Illumination changes, motion blur and occlusions further degrade both navigation
and visual inspection~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Wireless communication must finally
be considered, especially in multi-\gls{abb:uav} systems where drones need to
exchange information during the mission~\cite{acharya2020cornet}.

Against these constraints, the platform brings a set of capabilities that suit
the indoor setting. Multirotor \glspl{abb:uav} move in three dimensions and
hover, which lets them approach objects from different
positions~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned} and reach storage locations at different
heights without occupying floor space or requiring modifications to the
warehouse~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Their sensing is equally flexible, since cameras,
LiDAR and other sensors can be integrated according to the requirements of the
navigation and inspection tasks~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Operating autonomously,
however, requires the vehicle to detect obstacles, plan safe trajectories and
adapt its movement when the environment changes~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.

These capabilities are exploited in several indoor applications.
\Glspl{abb:uav} inspect infrastructure and industrial facilities while reducing
human exposure to repetitive or risky
tasks~\cite{tejeroruiz2024realistic, mohsan2023unmanned}, and monitor indoor
facilities and environmental conditions with cameras and other onboard
sensors~\cite{pore2026drone}. LiDAR and other depth sensors additionally support
three-dimensional mapping and obstacle detection~\cite{pore2026drone}. In
inventory inspection, the vehicle scans the codes placed on racks and feeds the
collected information into warehouse inventory
management~\cite{pore2026drone, kalinov2020warevision, cristiani2020inventory}.

\subsection{Multi-UAV Systems and Coordination}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-multi}

Working with a single platform imposes three limits on an inspection mission.
Energy comes first, since battery life directly limits how long a mission can
last~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}, and small \glspl{abb:uav} typically stay in
the air for only twenty to thirty minutes~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}. Coverage
follows from that endurance, because a single platform can inspect only one area
at a time, so the space it covers in one flight remains bounded. The mission is
finally vulnerable to failure: if the drone stops flying during the inspection,
the task ends there, since no other agent can take over from
it~\cite{chandran2024network}.

Deploying several platforms addresses these limits. The workload is divided
between the drones, and mission time decreases as the number of agents
grows~\cite{chandran2024network}; in a warehouse experiment, two \glspl{abb:uav}
halved the inspection time compared with a single drone~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.
Robustness and efficiency both improve, especially on complex
tasks~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}, and the redundancy brings fault tolerance,
since a drone that is lost can be detected by the others, which then replan their
paths so that the uncovered area is still inspected~\cite{chandran2024network}.
That redundancy stays under control, as coordination keeps the overlap low and
duplicate readings remained negligible in the same two-drone warehouse
experiment~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.

The literature distinguishes three families of coordination architecture, which
differ in where the decisions are taken and, as a consequence, in how much
computation and communication each drone has to carry.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Centralized}: All \glspl{abb:uav} communicate directly with a
          central control station, which handles map generation and path
          planning~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. This star topology minimises
          onboard computation but adds communication overhead, so a trade-off
          exists between network time and station computation
          time~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. The mission also depends on a
          single node, which becomes a point of
          failure~\cite{chandran2024network}.
    \item \textbf{Distributed}: The drones receive initial information from a
          central node, then decide on board~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. Each
          \gls{abb:uav} processes its own sensor data, builds a local map, plans
          its path, and keeps information about the others to allocate tasks
          efficiently~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. The cost is a higher
          computational and communication load, which makes energy efficiency
          critical~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}, and the network can split if a
          drone in the formation fails~\cite{chandran2024network}.
    \item \textbf{Decentralized}: Control is shared among the \glspl{abb:uav},
          which exchange information about their position and environment and
          decide collaboratively without a central control
          centre~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. This removes the single point of
          failure and lets the drones reconfigure their links when the topology
          changes~\cite{chandran2024network}. In exchange, it requires more
          sophisticated algorithms and communication
          protocols~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}, and local exchanges introduce
          latency that reduces responsiveness~\cite{chandran2024network}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{UAV Simulation Environments}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-simulation}

Simulation is an important step in \gls{abb:uav} research because real flight
experiments can be expensive, time-consuming, impractical, and sometimes
unsafe~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus}. A simulator allows researchers to develop and
test control, perception, and navigation algorithms in a virtual environment
while providing repeatable scenarios and access to the exact state of the
simulated vehicle, which can be used as a reference for
evaluation~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus, tejeroruiz2024realistic}. Simulation is also
widely used to generate data for learning-based methods and to train
reinforcement learning agents~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus}.

A \gls{abb:uav} simulator aims to reproduce the main elements involved in a real
flight:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Physics and vehicle dynamics}: The physics engine calculates
          the \gls{abb:uav}'s movement based on its structure and inertial
          properties~\cite{acharya2020cornet}. Accurately reproducing real flight
          dynamics remains one of the main challenges of aerial
          simulation~\cite{tejeroruiz2024realistic}.
    \item \textbf{Autopilot in the loop}: The simulator can be connected to
          flight-control software or hardware through \glsxtrfull{abb:sitl} and
          \glsxtrfull{abb:hitl} simulation~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus}. In
          \gls{abb:sitl}, the autopilot receives simulated sensor measurements and
          sends control commands back to the
          simulator~\cite{acharya2020cornet}.
    \item \textbf{Sensor models}: Simulation frameworks can reproduce sensors
          commonly used on \glspl{abb:uav}, such as \glspl{abb:imu}, barometers,
          magnetometers, \gls{abb:gps}, cameras, and
          \gls{abb:lidar}~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus, tejeroruiz2024realistic}.
          These models can include measurement characteristics and noise to make
          the simulated data closer to real sensor
          outputs~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus, tejeroruiz2024realistic}.
    \item \textbf{Rendering and visual fidelity}: Realistic rendering is
          important for vision-based navigation and perception algorithms because
          their performance depends directly on the generated
          images~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus}. Modern frameworks therefore use game
          engines to provide realistic environments and visual
          assets~\cite{tejeroruiz2024realistic}.
\end{itemize}

their physics engines, rendering capabilities, sensor support, and integration
with flight-control software. \Cref{tab:bg:simulators} compares the main

Despite its advantages, simulation cannot reproduce all the conditions of a real
flight. The main limitation is the simulation-to-reality gap, particularly for
visual perception, where lighting, occlusion, and sensor imperfections are
difficult to reproduce
accurately~\cite{pore2026uavqr, jacinto2024pegasus, tejeroruiz2024realistic}. For
example, one warehouse system achieved about 95 to 96\% \gls{abb:qr} detection
accuracy in simulation but only 86 to 90.5\% in real
experiments~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Photorealistic simulation also requires
significant computational resources, while complex simulation frameworks can
increase the development effort~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus, acharya2020cornet}.

\section{Warehouse Inventory Inspection and the Case for UAVs}%
\label{sec:bg:inspection}

\subsection{Warehouse Inventory Inspection}%
\label{subsec:bg:inspection-definition}

Inventory inspection is the operation that checks what is actually stored on the
shelves and compares it with the records kept by the \gls{abb:wms}. Its purpose
is to keep these two views consistent, because miscounts and misplaced items
directly affect order accuracy and raise operating costs~\cite{pore2026drone}.
The task is shaped by the environment in which it takes place. A typical
warehouse is a large building with many racks that can exceed ten metres in
height, where every pallet and every pallet position carries a unique identifier,
most often a barcode and more rarely an \gls{abb:rfid} or \gls{abb:qr}
marker~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}. Most facilities today are high-density,
large-scale storage sites, and as they keep growing in height and width,
obtaining timely inventory visibility without compromising operational safety has
become a real difficulty~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. In practice, the process is still
largely manual: a worker takes each pallet down, scans it, and puts it back,
which makes the operation repetitive and slow~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}. A
complete inventory check can consume more than three days of normal warehouse
activity~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}, so counting is usually performed
periodically rather than continuously, and managers have little visibility of
stock levels in real time~\cite{pore2026drone}.

\subsection{Traditional and Automated Inspection Methods}%
\label{subsec:bg:inspection-methods}

Four families of methods are used today to carry out this operation.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Manual inspection}: Operators walk through the aisles, read the
          labels, and update the records, sometimes still on
          paper~\cite{pore2026drone}. The method requires no special
          infrastructure, but it is time-consuming, labour-intensive, and exposed
          to human error~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.
    \item \textbf{Barcode and QR code scanning}: Optical identifiers are the
          dominant technology, and 1D barcodes alone are used in around 87\% of
          warehouses~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}. They are cheap to print, but
          reading them with a camera in real warehouse conditions is difficult,
          because of insufficient lighting, image artefacts produced by
          rolling-shutter cameras, and the small physical size of the
          codes~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}.
    \item \textbf{RFID systems}: Radio tags are the main alternative to optical
          codes. They require dedicated readers and infrastructure, whereas
          \gls{abb:qr} codes can be deployed without any~\cite{pore2026uavqr},
          and their performance degrades near metal racks because of multipath
          and signal attenuation~\cite{pore2026drone}.
    \item \textbf{Ground mobile robots (AGVs and AMRs)}: Scanners or cameras are
          mounted on a ground platform that moves along the aisles. These robots
          reduce manual work, but they stay on the floor plane, which limits
          their vertical reach, and they occupy floor space that competes with
          picking and forklift operations~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.
\end{itemize}

These four families differ in cost and in the degree of automation they offer,
but they share the same structural limits. None of them reaches the upper levels
of a high rack without either a lift or a human operator, each one requires
either a significant amount of manual work or dedicated infrastructure, and at
the scale of a full warehouse the inspection cycle is still measured in days
rather than hours~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, pore2026uavqr}.

\subsection{UAV-Based Inventory Inspection}%
\label{subsec:bg:inspection-uav}

\Glspl{abb:uav} address these limits directly. Because a drone moves freely in
three dimensions, it can reach storage locations that are otherwise inaccessible,
and it does so without requiring any structural change to the
building~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Compared with ground robots, it offers a better
vertical reach and interferes less with floor operations, which makes it
particularly suitable for dense warehouse layouts~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Turning
aisle traversal into an automated coverage problem also shortens the audit cycle
and allows high-frequency counting during shifts or at night, without scaling the
workforce in proportion~\cite{pore2026drone}. Two further benefits are reported:
sensing elevated rack levels no longer requires forklifts or scissor lifts, which
removes a fall hazard for the operator, and standardising flight speed, distance,
and scan geometry produces more repeatable observations than manual
scanning~\cite{pore2026drone}.

the identification technology they use, in the way the drone locates itself, in
the number of vehicles involved, and in how they are validated.

Three observations follow from this table, and a review of more than a hundred
warehouse-drone studies confirms them at a larger scale~\cite{pore2026drone}.
First, most systems use a single drone, and only one of those listed above
evaluates a second aerial vehicle~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. When several vehicles do
appear, they are heterogeneous rather than cooperating aerial agents: the ground
robot acts as a carrier or as a beacon deployer instead of a second
scanner~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, guinand2021uavugv}. Single-\gls{abb:uav}
systems remain clearly dominant in the literature, which makes scalability an
open research question~\cite{pore2026drone}. Second, validation is uneven. Only
one of these systems reports results in a real warehouse
testbed~\cite{pore2026uavqr}, while the others stay in the
laboratory~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, cristiani2020inventory} or in
simulation~\cite{alsulami2026deep}. At the scale of the whole field, about 45\%
of inventory-management studies are simulation-based against roughly 7\% that
report real experiments~\cite{pore2026drone}. Third, the environment is almost
always assumed static: only about 3\% of inventory studies consider a dynamic
warehouse with moving workers and vehicles~\cite{pore2026drone}. It is also worth
noting that most of these systems follow a pre-planned coverage path through
known aisles, rather than discovering the layout during the mission.

The inspection mission itself concentrates several difficulties that are still
not fully solved. Perception is the first one: vision-based reading of barcodes
and \gls{abb:qr} codes is sensitive to glare, low illumination, occlusion, motion
blur, and label orientation, and a code that cannot be decoded is an item that is
not counted~\cite{pore2026drone}. Navigation is the second: the warehouse is
\gls{abb:gps}-denied, so localisation must rely on onboard sensing or on
additional infrastructure, and the tight spacing between racks combined with
moving obstacles such as workers and forklifts forces conservative speed
planning~\cite{pore2026drone}. Energy is the third: the limited battery capacity
of a small platform caps the coverage achievable in one flight, which makes
docking and mission partitioning necessary in large
facilities~\cite{pore2026drone}. These constraints interact rather than adding
up, because on-board computation, memory, and endurance all limit how much
processing can be done during a long scanning mission~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. A
complete system must therefore guarantee reliable obstacle avoidance, sustain a
high decoding rate under variable illumination and partial occlusion, and keep
the inventory database synchronised, all at the same time~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.

\section{QR Codes and Their Reading from a UAV}%
\label{sec:bg:qr}

\subsection{QR Codes and the Difficulty of Reading Them from a UAV}%
\label{subsec:bg:qr-codes}

\Glspl{abb:qr} codes are two-dimensional visual identifiers that can store more
information than traditional barcodes and include error-correction mechanisms
that improve readability under partial
degradation~\cite{dong2024motionblurred, pore2026uavqr}. A \gls{abb:qr} code is
built from position detection patterns, format information, and data
modules~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}. The three square markers placed in the
corners have fixed proportions, which allows a decoder to locate the code and
correct its perspective, while Reed--Solomon error correction recovers the data
even when the symbol is partly hidden or blurred~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. These
properties make \gls{abb:qr} codes suitable for identifying items in warehouse
environments~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Reading them from a \gls{abb:uav} is more
difficult, however, because image quality changes continuously during flight.
Five factors degrade the image at that moment.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Distance and scale}: The code becomes too small to decode
          reliably when the \gls{abb:uav} is far from the
          target~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, alsulami2026deep}.
    \item \textbf{Viewing angle}: Labels may be observed from different angles,
          producing perspective
          distortion~\cite{pore2026drone, pore2026uavqr, alsulami2026deep}.
    \item \textbf{Motion blur}: \Gls{abb:uav} movement and vibration can blur the
          captured image and reduce recognition
          accuracy~\cite{dong2024motionblurred, kalinov2020warevision}.
    \item \textbf{Lighting}: Poor
          illumination~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, alsulami2026deep} and
          glare~\cite{pore2026drone} can significantly affect decoding
          performance.
    \item \textbf{Occlusion}: Shelves, packaging, or other objects may partially
          hide the code and prevent successful
          decoding~\cite{alsulami2026deep}.
\end{itemize}

These factors do not act separately: in flight they appear together, which
explains why results obtained in simulation do not transfer directly to a real
warehouse. One inventory system reached a \gls{abb:qr} accuracy of about 95 to
96\% in simulation but only 86 to 90.5\% in a real testbed, and the gap grew with
the complexity of the scenario, from around 5\% in the simplest case to more than
10\% in the hardest one~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. The authors of that work attribute
the difference to real lighting and occlusion conditions, and note that their
simulation stayed accurate for dynamics and coordination but not for optical
detection~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.

\subsection{QR Code Detection and Recognition Methods}%
\label{subsec:bg:qr-detection}

\Gls{abb:qr} code reading methods can be grouped into three main families.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Classical image processing}: These methods use hand-designed
          operations such as template matching, projection analysis, or
          segmentation~\cite{alsulami2026deep}. They are relatively simple, but
          they are sensitive to lighting, distortion, noise, and
          occlusion~\cite{alsulami2026deep}. Warehouse tests confirm these
          limits: one template-matching approach gave poor results at one metre
          and required a new set of patterns for each
          environment~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}.
    \item \textbf{Deep learning-based detection}: Neural networks learn visual
          features directly and are generally more robust to variations in scale,
          distortion, and partial occlusion~\cite{alsulami2026deep}.
          Architectures such as Faster R-CNN, SSD, and YOLO have been applied to
          this task~\cite{alsulami2026deep}. Recent \gls{abb:uav}-oriented work
          using YOLOv8 reported 92.4\% mAP on simulated images with
          millisecond-level inference, which makes this family suitable for
          real-time applications~\cite{alsulami2026deep}. Computational cost
          nevertheless remains an important constraint for onboard deployment,
          since some deep learning pipelines require more computing power than a
          drone can carry~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}.
    \item \textbf{Image restoration}: These approaches first enhance or restore
          degraded images before decoding. Recent methods use deep learning and
          generative models to reduce blur and improve \gls{abb:qr} recognition
          under difficult imaging conditions~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}.
          Processing time remains a limitation of this family, and reducing it is
          an explicit direction for future work~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Chapter Conclusion}%
\label{sec:bg:conclusion}

This chapter has introduced the elements needed to understand the rest of this
work. It first presented \glspl{abb:uav}, their categories, their components, the
specific conditions of indoor flight, the organisation of multi-\gls{abb:uav}
systems, and the simulation environments used to develop and test them. It then
described warehouse inventory inspection as an operational problem, reviewed the
methods used today, and examined the \gls{abb:uav}-based systems proposed in the
literature. It finally focused on \gls{abb:qr} codes and on the reasons why
reading them from a flying platform is difficult.

Several gaps appear from this review. Most existing inspection systems use a
single drone, and cooperation between several aerial agents remains
rare~\cite{pore2026drone}. The environment is almost always assumed to be
static, whereas a working warehouse contains moving operators and
vehicles~\cite{pore2026drone}. Above all, most systems follow a coverage path
that is planned in advance, in a layout that is already known. This assumption
is convenient, but it does not hold when the drone has to discover the
environment during the mission.

These gaps all point to the same layer of the system: the way the drone decides
where to go and when. Perception, identification, and platform design are
comparatively mature, but the navigation and decision layer is what determines
whether a drone can operate in an unknown and changing warehouse, and whether
several drones can share the work without repeating it. The next chapter
therefore examines this layer in detail. It compares the main families of
autonomous navigation approaches, separating those that plan a complete path
before the flight from those that revise their decisions as new observations
arrive, and it discusses what each family can and cannot offer for the mission
considered in this work.
