\section{Unmanned Aerial Vehicles}%
\label{sec:bg:uav}

\subsection{Definition of a UAV}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-definition}

An \glsxtrfull{abb:uav}, commonly called a drone, is an aircraft that operates
without a pilot on board. It can be controlled remotely by a human operator or
autonomously by software running on the vehicle. A \gls{abb:uav} is also equipped
with sensors selected according to its mission.\Glspl{abb:uav} are used in many fields, including surveillance, monitoring, search and rescue, and mapping~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive}. 


\subsection{Main Categories of UAVs}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-categories}

The literature generally distinguishes four main types of \glspl{abb:uav}:
fixed-wing, single-rotor, multirotor, and hybrid
platforms~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.

\textbf{Fixed-wing UAVs:} they use wings to generate lift and are designed for
efficient and fast flight. They offer long endurance and are therefore suitable
for large-area surveys and structure inspection.
However, they cannot hover and require a conventional take-off and landing
procedure. These constraints make them unsuitable for
confined indoor environments.

\textbf{Single-rotor UAVs:} they are similar to helicopters, can hover and carry
relatively large payloads. However, their mechanical
structure is more complex, and they are generally more expensive and difficult to
operate.

\textbf{Multirotor UAVs:} they are widely used for imaging and inspection because of their low cost, compact size, manoeuvrability, and ability to take off, land, and hover vertically. Quadrotors are the most common
configuration. Their main limitations are shorter flight time and lower payload
capacity compared with larger platforms.

\textbf{Hybrid UAVs:} they combine characteristics of fixed-wing and rotary-wing
\glspl{abb:uav}. They can perform vertical take-off and landing and can hover,
while also providing better flight efficiency than conventional
multirotors. However, their design and control are more
complex.

\subsubsection*{Suitability for Indoor Applications:}

Among these types, multirotor \glspl{abb:uav} are the most suitable for indoor
inspection. They can hover and move freely in three dimensions, which lets them
approach objects closely, and their compact size
allows them to fly in narrow aisles and reach different rack levels. The main difficulty is autonomous control, since the drone must plan and follow its path safely in a confined space.

\subsubsection*{Airframe and Propulsion System}

The airframe carries every other subsystem, while the propulsion chain converts
electrical energy into thrust. The elements below follow the description given
in~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Frame}: Provides the mechanical structure and supports the
          other components.
    \item \textbf{Motors}: Convert electrical energy into rotation to drive the
          propellers.
    \item \textbf{Propellers}: Generate the thrust needed to lift the vehicle
          and move it. Their number and arrangement define the multirotor
          configuration, with quadrotors being the most common.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Flight Control System}

This subsystem closes the loop between what the sensors measure and what the
motors do. Its three levels are described in~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Flight controller}: Processes the sensor measurements and
          produces the commands that keep the vehicle stable.
    \item \textbf{Autopilot}: Handles mission planning and waypoint generation.
          PX4 and ArduPilot are the most widely used open-source
          stacks~\cite{acharya2020cornet}.
    \item \textbf{Low-level control}: Regulates altitude, velocity and
          orientation so that the vehicle follows the requested trajectory.
          \Gls{abb:pid} controllers are common here because they are simple to
          implement.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Onboard Sensors}

Two groups of sensors are used: one estimates the state of the vehicle, the
other observes the environment. The following description draws
on~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus, pore2026drone}.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{IMU}: Combines an accelerometer and a gyroscope to measure
          linear acceleration and angular motion.
    \item \textbf{Barometer and magnetometer}: Provide altitude and heading
          respectively.
    \item \textbf{GNSS/GPS}: Provides global positioning outdoors. Indoors the
          signal is unreliable, so other methods are
          required~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.
    \item \textbf{RGB camera}: Provides the images used to detect and read
          barcodes and \gls{abb:qr} codes.
    \item \textbf{Depth camera and LiDAR}: Provide distance measurements for
          mapping and obstacle detection~\cite{chang2023review}.
    \item \textbf{ToF sensors}: Measure distance from the travel time of a
          signal, with accuracy decreasing as the distance
          grows~\cite{tejeroruiz2024realistic}, which makes them suited to
          short-range proximity detection.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Onboard Computing and Communication}

Perception and decision are kept apart from flight control, so that a heavy
computation cannot affect the stability of the vehicle. The architecture below
follows~\cite{acharya2020cornet}.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Companion computer}: Runs the high-level logic of the mission
          and sends the resulting commands to the flight control unit.
    \item \textbf{Wi-Fi data link}: Carries the collected data to the ground
          station and, in multi-\gls{abb:uav} systems, to the other
          drones~\cite{chandran2024network}.
    \item \textbf{Telemetry radio}: Carries the flight status and the control
          messages exchanged with the ground station.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{UAVs in Indoor Environments}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-indoor}

Indoor flight differs from outdoor flight in several ways. Warehouses are
confined spaces, built around narrow aisles and multi-level racks that leave
little room to manoeuvre. They also contain obstacles that move, since operators
and forklifts share the same aisles, so the vehicle must detect and avoid them
during the flight. Poor lighting, motion blur and occlusions degrade both
navigation and the reading of codes~\cite{pore2026drone, pore2026uavqr}.
Positioning is a further difficulty, because the \gls{abb:gnss} signal is
unreliable indoors and the vehicle must therefore estimate its position from its
own sensors~\cite{chang2023review}.

Multirotor platforms answer these constraints well. They hover and move in three
dimensions, which lets them approach an object from any
position~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}, and they reach storage locations at
different heights without occupying floor space or requiring any change to the
building~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Flying autonomously, however, still requires the
vehicle to detect obstacles, plan a safe trajectory and adapt its movement when
the environment changes.


\subsection{Multi-UAV Systems and Coordination}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-multi}

A single platform imposes three limits on an inspection mission. Energy comes
first, since battery life directly bounds how long a mission can last, and small
\glspl{abb:uav} typically stay in the air for only twenty to thirty
minutes~\cite{mohsan2023unmanned}. Coverage follows from that endurance, because
one vehicle inspects a single area at a time, so the space it covers in one
flight remains bounded. The mission is also vulnerable to failure: if the drone
stops flying, the task ends there, since no other agent can take over from
it~\cite{chandran2024network}.

Deploying several platforms answers these limits. The workload is divided between
the drones, so mission time decreases as the team grows, and the loss of one
member no longer ends the mission, because the others detect it and replan their
paths to cover the area it was inspecting~\cite{chandran2024network}. The gain is
measurable: in a warehouse experiment, two \glspl{abb:uav} halved the inspection
time of a single drone while keeping duplicate readings
negligible~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.

The literature distinguishes three families of coordination architecture, which
differ in where the decisions are taken and, as a result, in how much computation
and communication each drone has to carry. The description below draws
on~\cite{kumar2025comprehensive, chandran2024network}.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Centralized}: All \glspl{abb:uav} communicate with a central
          station, which builds the map and plans the paths. This minimises
          onboard computation but adds communication overhead, and the whole
          mission depends on a single node, which becomes a point of failure.
    \item \textbf{Distributed}: The drones receive initial information from a
          central node, then decide on board. Each one processes its own sensor
          data, builds a local map and plans its path, which raises the
          computational and communication load. The network can also split if a
          drone in the formation fails.
    \item \textbf{Decentralized}: Control is shared among the drones, which
          exchange information about their position and environment and decide
          together, without any central station. This removes the single point of
          failure and lets them reconfigure their links when the topology
          changes, but it requires more complex algorithms and introduces latency
          in the decisions.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{UAV Simulation Environments}%
\label{subsec:bg:uav-simulation}

Simulation is an important step in \gls{abb:uav} research, because real flight
experiments are expensive, slow, and sometimes unsafe. A simulator lets
researchers develop and test control, perception and navigation algorithms in a
virtual environment, with repeatable scenarios and access to the exact state of
the vehicle, which serves as a reference for evaluation. It is also the usual way
to produce the data needed to train learning-based
methods~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus}.

A simulator aims to reproduce four elements of a real
flight~\cite{jacinto2024pegasus, tejeroruiz2024realistic}.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Physics and vehicle dynamics}: A physics engine computes the
          motion of the vehicle from its structure and its inertial properties.
          Reproducing real flight dynamics accurately remains the main
          difficulty of aerial simulation.
    \item \textbf{Autopilot in the loop}: The simulator can be connected to the
          firmware of a real flight controller through \glsxtrfull{abb:sitl} and
          \glsxtrfull{abb:hitl} simulation. In \gls{abb:sitl}, the autopilot
          receives the simulated measurements and sends back the motor
          commands~\cite{acharya2020cornet}.
    \item \textbf{Sensor models}: Frameworks reproduce the sensors found on a
          real platform, such as the \gls{abb:imu}, the barometer, the
          magnetometer, \gls{abb:gps}, cameras and \gls{abb:lidar}, and they can
          add noise to bring the simulated data closer to real measurements.
    \item \textbf{Rendering}: Visual quality matters for any algorithm that
          works on images, which is why recent frameworks rely on game engines.
\end{itemize}

rendering quality, the sensors they simulate, and their integration with
flight-control software. \Cref{tab:bg:simulators} compares the main ones.

\section{Warehouse Inventory Inspection and the Case for UAVs}%
\label{sec:bg:inspection}

\subsection{Warehouse Inventory Inspection}%
\label{subsec:bg:inspection-definition}

Inventory inspection checks what is actually stored on the shelves and compares
it with the records kept by the \gls{abb:wms}. Keeping these two views consistent
matters, because miscounts and misplaced items reduce order accuracy and raise
operating costs~\cite{pore2026drone}. The environment makes the task difficult. A
typical warehouse is a large building with racks that can exceed ten metres in
height, and every pallet position carries a unique identifier, most often a
barcode. The process is also still largely manual: a worker takes each pallet
down, scans it, and puts it back, and a complete check can consume more than
three days of normal warehouse activity~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}. Counting is
therefore periodic rather than continuous, which leaves managers with little
real-time visibility of stock levels~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.

\subsection{Traditional and Automated Inspection Methods}%
\label{subsec:bg:inspection-methods}

Four families of methods are used today.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Manual inspection}: Operators walk through the aisles, read the
          labels, and update the records. The method needs no special
          infrastructure, but it is slow, labour-intensive, and exposed to human
          error~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.
    \item \textbf{Barcode and QR code scanning}: Optical identifiers dominate,
          and 1D barcodes alone are used in around 87\% of warehouses. They are
          cheap to print, but camera-based reading is difficult in real
          conditions because of poor lighting, rolling-shutter artefacts, and the
          small size of the codes~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}.
    \item \textbf{RFID systems}: Radio tags require dedicated readers and
          infrastructure, whereas \gls{abb:qr} codes need
          none~\cite{pore2026uavqr}, and their performance degrades near metal
          racks because of multipath and attenuation~\cite{pore2026drone}.
    \item \textbf{Ground mobile robots (AGVs and AMRs)}: These reduce manual
          work, but they stay on the floor plane, which limits their vertical
          reach, and they occupy space that competes with picking
          operations~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.
\end{itemize}

The four families share the same structural limits: none reaches the top of a
high rack without a lift or an operator, each one needs either manual work or
dedicated infrastructure, and at warehouse scale the inspection cycle is still
measured in days~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, pore2026uavqr}.

\subsection{UAV-Based Inventory Inspection}%
\label{subsec:bg:inspection-uav}

\Glspl{abb:uav} answer these limits directly. A drone reaches storage locations
that are otherwise inaccessible, without any structural change to the building,
and it interferes less with floor operations than a ground
robot~\cite{pore2026uavqr}. Treating aisle traversal as an automated coverage
problem also shortens the audit cycle and allows frequent counting without
increasing the workforce. Two further benefits are reported: elevated racks no
longer require forklifts or scissor lifts, which removes a fall hazard, and
standardising flight speed and scan geometry produces more repeatable
observations than manual scanning~\cite{pore2026drone}.

Three observations follow from this table, and a review of more than a hundred
warehouse-drone studies confirms them at a larger scale~\cite{pore2026drone}.

First, single-\gls{abb:uav} systems dominate. Only one of the systems listed
above evaluates a second aerial vehicle~\cite{pore2026uavqr}, and where several
vehicles appear, the ground robot is a carrier or a beacon deployer rather than a
second scanner~\cite{kalinov2020warevision, guinand2021uavugv}. Scalability
therefore remains an open question.

Second, validation is uneven. Only one system reports results in a real warehouse
testbed~\cite{pore2026uavqr}, and across the field about 45\% of
inventory-management studies are simulation-based against roughly 7\% with real
experiments.

Third, the environment is almost always assumed static, since only about 3\% of
studies consider a dynamic warehouse with moving workers and vehicles. Most
systems also follow a pre-planned coverage path through known aisles rather than
discovering the layout during the mission.
\section{QR Codes and Their Reading from a UAV}%
\label{sec:bg:qr}

\subsection{Why Reading a QR Code from a UAV Is Difficult}%
\label{subsec:bg:qr-codes}

A \gls{abb:qr} code is a two-dimensional identifier that stores more data than a
barcode and includes an error-correction mechanism, so it can still be decoded
when the symbol is partly hidden or blurred~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}. A code
is only decodable, however, within a bounded range of distances, viewing angles
and relative speeds. Five factors push the image outside that range during
flight.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Distance}: The code becomes too small to decode when the
          vehicle is far from the target~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}.
    \item \textbf{Viewing angle}: Labels are rarely seen head-on, which distorts
          the symbol~\cite{alsulami2026deep}.
    \item \textbf{Motion blur}: The movement and the vibration of the platform
          blur the captured image~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}.
    \item \textbf{Lighting}: Poor illumination and glare degrade
          decoding~\cite{pore2026drone}.
    \item \textbf{Occlusion}: Shelves or packaging may hide part of the
          code~\cite{alsulami2026deep}.
\end{itemize}

These factors act together, which is why simulation results do not transfer
directly to a real warehouse. One inventory system reached about 95 to 96\%
accuracy in simulation but only 86 to 90.5\% in a real testbed, a gap its authors
attribute to real lighting and occlusion~\cite{pore2026uavqr}.

\subsection{QR Code Detection and Recognition Methods}%
\label{subsec:bg:qr-detection}

Three families of methods are used to read these codes.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Classical image processing}: Hand-designed operations such as
          template matching or segmentation. They are simple but fail in real
          warehouse conditions: one template-matching approach gave poor results
          at one metre and needed a new set of patterns for each
          environment~\cite{kalinov2020warevision}.
    \item \textbf{Deep learning detection}: Neural networks learn visual features
          directly and tolerate scale changes, distortion and partial occlusion.
          A recent YOLOv8-based system reached 92.4\% mAP with millisecond-level
          inference, which makes this family usable in real time. Its cost is the
          computing power it requires on board~\cite{alsulami2026deep}.
    \item \textbf{Image restoration}: The degraded image is enhanced before
          decoding, usually with generative models that remove blur. The added
          processing time is the main
          limitation~\cite{dong2024motionblurred}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}%
\label{sec:bg:conclusion}

This chapter has introduced the elements needed to understand the rest of this
work. It first presented \glspl{abb:uav}, their categories, their components, the
specific conditions of indoor flight, the organisation of multi-\gls{abb:uav}
systems, and the simulation environments used to develop and test them. It then
described warehouse inventory inspection as an operational problem, reviewed the
methods used today, and examined the \gls{abb:uav}-based systems proposed in the
literature. It finally focused on \gls{abb:qr} codes and on the reasons why
reading them from a flying platform is difficult.

Several gaps appear from this review. Most existing inspection systems use a
single drone, and cooperation between several aerial agents remains
rare~\cite{pore2026drone}. The environment is almost always assumed to be
static, whereas a working warehouse contains moving operators and
vehicles~\cite{pore2026drone}. Above all, most systems follow a coverage path
that is planned in advance, in a layout that is already known. This assumption
is convenient, but it does not hold when the drone has to discover the
environment during the mission.

These gaps all point to the same layer of the system: the way the drone decides
where to go and when. Perception, identification, and platform design are
comparatively mature, but the navigation and decision layer is what determines
whether a drone can operate in an unknown and changing warehouse, and whether
several drones can share the work without repeating it. The next chapter
therefore examines this layer in detail. It compares the main families of
autonomous navigation approaches, separating those that plan a complete path
before the flight from those that revise their decisions as new observations
arrive, and it discusses what each family can and cannot offer for the mission
considered in this work.
